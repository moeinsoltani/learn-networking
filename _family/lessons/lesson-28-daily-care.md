# Lesson 28: Daily Care

{: .important }
> General education, not medical advice. When something about your baby's skin,
> cord, or care worries you, ask your provider — and see Lesson 30 for red flags.

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

The daily-care basics — **diapering, bathing, cord care, dressing, and handling** —
are quickly learned and soon become second nature. Most of it is simpler than new
parents fear, and many "problems" (dry skin, **baby acne**[°](#w-baby-acne), **cradle cap**[°](#w-cradle-cap)) are **normal and
self-resolving**. This lesson gives you the confidence to handle the everyday
looking-after without second-guessing every step.

## Going Deeper

### Diapering

Change diapers **often** (whenever wet or soiled — newborns need many changes a
day). Wipe front-to-back (especially for girls), let the skin dry, and use a barrier
cream if needed. **Prevent diaper rash** with frequent changes, gentle cleaning, and
some nappy-free air time; most rashes clear with these steps, but ask if it's severe,
blistering, or not improving. The **cord stump** should be kept outside the diaper
(fold the diaper down) to stay dry.

### Bathing

Newborns don't need daily baths — **2–3 times a week** is plenty (more drying is
worse for their skin). Until the **cord stump falls off**, stick to **sponge baths**[°](#w-sponge-bath).
Then:

- Use **warm (not hot) water** — test with your elbow/wrist — and support the head
  and neck at all times.
- **Never leave a baby alone** in or near water, even for a second.
- Keep baths short; pat dry, especially the skin folds.

### Umbilical cord care

Keep the stump **clean and dry**; it falls off on its own in about **1–3 weeks**. A
little dried blood when it separates is normal. **Contact your provider** if you see
signs of infection: **redness spreading** around the base, **pus/oozing**, a bad
smell, or if the baby has a fever.

### Dressing, nails, and skin

- **Dress** the baby in about **one more layer than you'd wear** — comfortable, not
  overheated (overheating is a safe-sleep concern, Lesson 25).
- **Nails** grow fast and babies scratch themselves — file or carefully trim them
  (easiest when the baby is asleep).
- **Skin:** dry/peeling skin, **baby acne**, and **cradle cap** are all common and
  harmless and pass on their own; you don't need to treat them aggressively.

### Handling

Always **support the head and neck** (newborns can't yet). Handle the **soft spots**
gently — but they're covered by a tough membrane and are more robust than they look.
Skin-to-skin, gentle rocking, and talking are all part of care, not just cleaning.

{: .note }
> **Most "problems" are normal**
> Cradle cap, baby acne, dry peeling skin, occasional spit-up, and mild diaper
> redness are usually normal and self-limiting. Save your worry (and your calls) for
> the things that actually signal trouble — spreading redness or pus at the cord, a
> severe or blistering rash, or the Lesson 30 red flags.

### Where to start

Practise the basics early, and practise them **both**: a diaper change, supporting
the head, the sponge-bath routine, dressing a floppy newborn. Awkwardness on the
first few attempts is universal and passes quickly.

Learn the **cord-care signs** together as well — what healing looks like versus
the redness, swelling, smell, or discharge that means call someone.

Then agree how you will split daily care so it does not silently default to one
person. This matters more than it sounds: when one parent becomes the sole
expert, the other cannot take over, and the expert never gets to rest. Two
confident caregivers is the actual goal here, not one very practised one.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** How often do newborns need baths, and what's the rule until the cord falls
off?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
About <strong>2–3 times a week</strong> is plenty (frequent bathing dries their
skin). Until the <strong>umbilical cord stump falls off</strong> (~1–3 weeks), use
<strong>sponge baths</strong> rather than immersing the baby. Always use warm water,
support the head, and never leave them alone near water.
</details>

**Q2.** How do you care for the cord stump, and what signs of infection mean "call"?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Keep it <strong>clean and dry</strong> (fold the diaper down so it's exposed); it
falls off on its own in ~1–3 weeks. Call the provider for signs of
<strong>infection</strong>: <strong>spreading redness</strong> around the base,
<strong>pus/oozing</strong>, a bad smell, or a fever.
</details>

**Q3.** Name two harmless newborn skin things that don't need aggressive treatment.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Any two: <strong>cradle cap</strong> (flaky scalp patches), <strong>baby acne</strong>,
and <strong>dry/peeling skin</strong> — all common, harmless, and self-resolving.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-umbilical-cord-stump"></a>**umbilical cord stump** — the small remainder of the cord; it dries and falls off in ~1–3 weeks.
- <a id="w-diaper-rash"></a>**diaper rash** — sore, red skin in the diaper area; usually preventable and treatable.
- <a id="w-sponge-bath"></a>**sponge bath** — washing with a damp cloth (used until the cord falls off).
- <a id="w-cradle-cap"></a>**cradle cap** — flaky, scaly patches on the scalp; harmless and common.
- <a id="w-baby-acne"></a>**baby acne** — small bumps on a newborn's face; harmless and passes.
- <a id="w-fontanelle"></a>**fontanelle** — a soft spot on the head; handle gently, but it's tougher than it looks.
