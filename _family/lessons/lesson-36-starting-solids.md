# Lesson 36: Starting Solids

{: .important }
> General education, not medical advice. Timing, allergens, and any special
> circumstances should be confirmed with your provider — especially if there's a
> family history of allergies or eczema.

{: .note }
> **Words to know**
> - **solids / complementary foods** — foods other than milk, started around 6 months.
> - **weaning** — the process of introducing foods alongside (not instantly replacing) milk.
> - **baby-led weaning** — letting the baby self-feed soft finger foods (vs. spoon-fed purées).
> - **allergen** — a food that can cause an allergic reaction (peanut, egg, dairy, etc.).
> - **gagging vs. choking** — gagging is a normal, noisy safety reflex; choking is silent and dangerous.
> - **tongue-thrust reflex** — the early reflex that pushes food out; it fades when a baby is ready for solids.

## Concept

Around **6 months**, milk alone stops being enough and babies start **solid foods**
— but in the first year, **milk (breast or formula) is still the main nutrition**;
early solids are about **learning, tasting, and topping up key nutrients (especially
iron)**, not replacing milk. It's messy, gradual, and meant to be exploratory.
Approach it relaxed: **you're teaching a skill, not hitting a quota.**

## Going Deeper

### When to start — readiness, not just age

Aim for **around 6 months**, guided by **signs of readiness**: the baby can **sit
with support and hold their head steady**, shows **interest in food**, and has lost
the **tongue-thrust reflex** (no longer automatically pushing food out). Not before
~4 months. If unsure, ask your provider.

### How — purées, baby-led, or both

- **Spoon-fed purées:** smooth foods, gradually thicker and lumpier.
- **Baby-led weaning:** soft finger foods the baby self-feeds.
- **A mix of both** is completely fine — there's no single right method.

Start with **iron-rich foods** (iron-fortified cereal, puréed or soft meat, beans,
lentils) plus vegetables and fruit — **iron matters** because babies' stores run low
by ~6 months. Offer **one new food at a time** early on so you can spot reactions,
keep textures appropriate, and let the baby explore (mess is learning).

### Allergens — introduce early and regularly

Current guidance is to **introduce common allergens early (from ~6 months) and keep
offering them regularly** — including **peanut** (as smooth peanut butter/thinned,
never whole nuts) and **egg**, plus dairy, wheat, fish, etc. **Early, regular
exposure lowers allergy risk.** If your baby has severe eczema or a known family
allergy history, **talk to your provider first** about how to introduce these
safely.

### Safety: choking, and foods to avoid

- **Gagging is normal** (a loud, protective reflex as babies learn); **choking is
  silent** and an emergency — **always supervise** meals, seat the baby upright,
  and **learn infant choking first aid / CPR** (Lesson 39).
- **Avoid choking hazards:** whole nuts, whole grapes/cherry tomatoes (cut small),
  hard raw chunks, popcorn, and similar — modify textures to be safe.
- **Don't give:** **honey before 12 months** (risk of infant botulism), **added
  salt or sugar**, or **cow's milk as the main drink** before 12 months (small
  amounts in cooking are fine).

{: .note }
> **First year: food is for fun and learning**
> "Food before one is just for fun" is a useful (if simplified) reminder — milk
> stays the main nutrition this year, so don't stress about how *much* solid food
> the baby eats. Focus on iron-rich foods, exposure to lots of tastes and textures
> (including allergens, early and often), and safe, supervised, relaxed mealtimes.

### Where to start

When the time approaches, watch for the **readiness signs** rather than the
calendar: sitting with good head control, losing the reflex that pushes food back
out, and showing real interest in what you are eating. Around six months is the
guide, not the trigger.

Plan a few **iron-rich first foods**, because iron is the actual nutritional
reason for starting when you do — the stores a baby is born with run low around
this point.

Make a simple plan to **introduce common allergens early and safely**, one at a
time — this is the reversal of the old advice, and it is well supported. If there
is a family history of allergy or eczema, check the approach with your provider
first.

And if you have not yet, find an **infant first-aid and choking course** (Lesson
39). Starting solids is the natural moment to have that knowledge already in
place rather than intending to get it.

The whole recipe, in one line: relaxed, exploratory, iron-focused,
allergen-inclusive, and supervised.
