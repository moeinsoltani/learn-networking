# Lesson 30: Newborn Health & Warning Signs

{: .important }
> General education, not medical advice. This lesson names red flags so you act
> fast — but it is **not** a diagnosis tool. For a young baby, **trust your
> instincts and don't wait**; a fever in a baby under 3 months is an emergency.

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

Most newborn health is about **routine care** — **well-baby visits**[°](#w-well-baby-visit), vaccines, and
growth tracking — punctuated by a few **common, usually-manageable issues** (like
**jaundice**[°](#w-jaundice)) and a short list of **genuine red flags** that mean *act now*. Knowing the
red flags cold is the goal of this lesson: for a young baby, some symptoms are true
emergencies, and hesitating "not to overreact" is the wrong instinct. **When in
doubt, call — or go in.**

## Going Deeper

### Routine care

- **Well-baby visits** track weight, length, head size, feeding, and development,
  and are your chance to ask questions.
- **Immunisations** begin in the first months on a schedule — they protect against
  serious diseases; keep to the schedule.
- **Growth** is followed on charts; steady growth along a curve matters more than a
  single number.

### Common issues

- **Jaundice:** yellowing of the skin/whites of the eyes, from **bilirubin**, is
  **very common** in the first days and often mild. But it can occasionally need
  treatment (light therapy), so **have it checked** — especially if it appears in
  the first 24 hours, spreads/deepens, or the baby is sleepy and feeding poorly.
- **Feeding and weight** worries, **cord** issues (Lesson 28), and mild reflux/
  spit-up are common — ask if unsure.

### The newborn red flags — act now

{: .warning }
> **Get urgent medical help / go to emergency if your baby:**
> - Has a **fever of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or higher and is under 3 months old** —
>   **this is an emergency; go now.**
> - Has **trouble breathing** (fast, laboured, grunting, pauses, or blue lips/skin).
> - Is **very hard to wake, floppy, or unusually unresponsive** (**lethargy**[°](#w-lethargy)).
> - **Won't feed** or is feeding very poorly, or has **far fewer wet diapers**
>   (possible **dehydration**[°](#w-dehydration)).
> - Has **persistent or forceful vomiting**, especially **green** vomit, or **blood
>   in stool/vomit**.
> - Is **inconsolable** in a way that feels different and wrong, or has a **rash that
>   doesn't fade** when pressed.
> - Has a **seizure**, or you simply feel **something is seriously wrong** — trust it.

### Taking a temperature, and trusting yourself

Learn to take your baby's temperature (a **rectal** reading is most accurate for a
young infant; your provider can show you). And remember the golden rule: **you know
your baby.** Parental instinct that "something is off" is a valid reason to seek
care. Health professionals would always rather see a well baby than miss a sick one.

{: .note }
> **The one number to burn into memory**
> **Fever ≥ 38 °C (100.4 °F) in a baby under 3 months = emergency, go now.** A young
> baby's immune system is immature, so a fever can signal a serious infection and
> needs urgent assessment — never "wait and see" with a feverish newborn.

### Where to start

**Both of you** memorise the red-flag list, and above all the one rule that
allows no hesitation: **a fever of 38 °C or higher in a baby under three months
means go now.**

Learn how to take the baby's temperature properly before you need to, and save
the numbers you would want at 2 a.m. — your provider, the after-hours line, and
the nearest emergency department. Post the red flags somewhere visible; the
fridge is not too undignified for this.

The purpose of all of it is that in a frightening moment you are not looking
things up. You already know what counts as an emergency and exactly who to call.
And one more thing worth hearing plainly: **your instinct to seek help is always
valid.** No clinician will think less of you for bringing in a baby who turns out
to be fine.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** What's the emergency rule about fever in a young baby?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
A <strong>fever of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or higher in a baby under 3 months old is an
emergency — seek care immediately (go to emergency).</strong> A young baby's immune
system is immature, so a fever can signal a serious infection and must never be left
to "wait and see."
</details>

**Q2.** What is jaundice, and when should it prompt a check?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
<strong>Jaundice</strong> is yellowing of the skin and eye-whites from bilirubin,
very common in newborns and often mild. Have it checked — especially if it appears in
the <strong>first 24 hours</strong>, spreads or deepens, or the baby is
<strong>sleepy and feeding poorly</strong>; it occasionally needs light therapy.
</details>

**Q3.** Besides fever, name three newborn red flags that mean "seek urgent help."

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Any three: <strong>trouble breathing</strong> (fast/grunting/pauses/blue lips);
<strong>very hard to wake or floppy</strong> (lethargy); <strong>won't feed or far
fewer wet diapers</strong>; <strong>persistent/green vomiting or blood in stool</strong>;
a <strong>non-fading rash</strong>; a <strong>seizure</strong>; or a strong instinct
that something is seriously wrong.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-well-baby-visit"></a>**well-baby visit** — a routine checkup to track growth, development, and health.
- <a id="w-immunisation-vaccine"></a>**immunisation / vaccine** — protection against serious infections; the schedule starts in the first months.
- <a id="w-jaundice"></a>**jaundice** (JAWN-diss) — yellowing of the skin/eyes from bilirubin; common in newborns, sometimes needs treatment.
- <a id="w-rectal-temperature"></a>**rectal temperature** — the most accurate way to take a young baby's temperature.
- <a id="w-lethargy"></a>**lethargy** — being unusually drowsy, floppy, or hard to wake.
- <a id="w-dehydration"></a>**dehydration** — not enough fluids; watch wet-diaper counts.
