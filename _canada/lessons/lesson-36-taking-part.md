# Lesson 36: Taking Part

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

Phase 5 ended on a finding this lesson operationalizes: **Canada's
institutions are guaranteed by civic culture, not text** — the
conventions hold because people show up. This lesson is the showing-up
manual. Every Canadian (and every resident — most participation
doesn't require citizenship) holds a **three-level toolkit**:

Lesson 29's map of who does what becomes much more useful once you turn it
into a list of people you can actually contact.

| Level | Your representative | What they can help with |
|---|---|---|
| **Federal** | Your **MP** — and note that a riding office does real **casework**[°](#w-casework), especially on immigration files | Immigration, taxes, EI, criminal law, foreign affairs, First Nations matters |
| **Provincial** | Your **MPP, MLA, or MNA** (the title varies by province) | Health care, schools, housing law, licences, employment standards |
| **Municipal** | Your **councillor**, the mayor, and your **school trustee**[°](#w-school-trustee) | Zoning, transit, the police board, parks, permits |

Two practical observations. First, **the municipal level answers emails and
changes fastest** — it is the least glamorous tier and by far the most
responsive, and a single well-argued **deputation**[°](#w-deputation) at a city committee can
genuinely change an outcome. Second, **voting requires citizenship, but almost
nothing else does.** Contacting any of the representatives above, speaking at
city hall, joining public consultations, sitting on a school council, holding
a party membership (most Canadian parties accept non-citizens), volunteering,
protesting, signing **petitions**[°](#w-petition), joining a community board or a union — all of
it is open to permanent residents from the day they arrive.

The central, verifiable claim: **contacting representatives works
far more often than cynics believe** — not because politicians are
saints, but because of an asymmetry: almost nobody writes.
A riding office receiving *twelve* individually written letters on
one issue treats it as a groundswell; a councillor can be moved by
five deputations. In a system run on attention (Lesson 27's PMO,
Lesson 26's QP), scarce citizen attention is disproportionately
valuable. The cynic's non-participation is the participant's
discount.

## Going Deeper

### Voting, three ways (and the turnout ladder)

Federal (Lesson 28: Elections Canada, FPTP, ~60–67% turnout);
provincial (same FPTP logic, separate agencies and dates, ~40–60%);
municipal (varies wildly — often ~30–40%, the lowest turnout for
the government with the most daily-life surface: the standing
civic scandal). School-board trustees appear on municipal ballots
— the most ignored elected office in the country, wielding real
curriculum-adjacent and budget power (*and*, per Lesson 34,
schools are where the state meets your kids). The arithmetic
insight: **your vote's marginal weight rises as turnout falls** —
a municipal vote is, influence-per-ballot, several federal votes.

### The casework secret: what riding offices actually do

Every MP and provincial member runs a **constituency office**
whose main job isn't politics — it's **casework**: unsticking
constituents' files with the bureaucracy. Immigration applications
lost in queues, EI disputes, CRA tangles, passport emergencies,
veterans' benefits — riding staff phone dedicated
government-liaison lines that you cannot reach, and things move.
This service is free, non-partisan in practice (offices help
constituents regardless of how they voted — the incentive is
reputation), and *available to non-citizens* (an immigration file
is the classic case). The course's most practically valuable
sentence for a newcomer might be: **when your file is stuck past
published processing times, email your MP's office with your file
number.**

### The engagement ladder, bottom to top

1. **Inform yourself** — you've done 36 lessons of this; add: your
   three representatives' names (this lesson's lab), one local
   news source (local journalism's collapse is a real civic
   problem — subscribing is participation).
2. **Contact** — letters/emails to the right level (Lesson 29's
   routing table); individually written beats petitions
   100-signatures-to-one; ask for a response; be brief, specific,
   constituent-identified.
3. **Show up** — city-council deputations (you can address
   council directly — sign-up is a form; five minutes at the
   mic enters the record); public consultations (zoning,
   transit, budgets — attended by nine people, hence powerful);
   **town halls**[°](#w-town-hall); school councils.
4. **Join** — party membership (usually ~$10–25, open to PRs in
   most parties: members choose candidates and leaders — the
   **nomination contest**[°](#w-nomination-contest), not the election, decides safe seats:
   the highest-leverage vote in Canadian politics that almost
   nobody casts); unions and professional associations;
   advocacy organizations.
5. **Volunteer** — Canada's quiet superpower: roughly 4 in 10
   Canadians formally volunteer (billions of hours — food
   banks, coaching, settlement agencies helping the next
   newcomer, volunteer fire departments protecting much of
   rural Canada, the **CVITP**[°](#w-cvitp) tax clinics of Lesson 35). It's also
   the established on-ramp for newcomers: Canadian references,
   Canadian-workplace fluency (English track, Phase 4!), and
   the network that answers Lesson 41's "Canadian experience"
   catch-22.
6. **Protest** — constitutionally protected (s.2's assembly and
   expression, Lesson 31, within the usual limits); Canada's
   protest tradition is real (Winnipeg 1919 → Idle No More →
   climate strikes), and its edge cases (rail blockades, the
   2022 convoy and the Emergencies Act's first use) are where
   Lesson 31's balancing gets stress-tested live.
7. **Run** — city councils and school boards are the open
   door: winnable with door-knocking and a few thousand
   dollars; most MPs started exactly there.

### Duties that aren't optional

The census (mandatory, and StatsCan will pursue you — the data
runs equalization, seats, and services); jury duty (Lesson 33);
and — cultural but near-universal — the poppy fortnight
(Lesson 18), Orange Shirt Day observance (Lesson 24), and
shovelling your sidewalk (municipal **bylaw**[°](#w-bylaw) *and* moral law;
Lesson 03's climate making neighbourliness compulsory).

{: .note }
> **Participation without citizenship — the PR's full menu**
> Everything except voting and running: contact any office
> (casework explicitly included), depute at council, join most
> parties (and thus vote in nominations/leaderships — genuinely
> odd and true: a PR can help choose a future PM before they
> can vote for an MP), sit on municipal advisory committees,
> organize, donate (citizens and PRs only — corporate money is
> banned, Lesson 28), volunteer everywhere. The design logic:
> residence is the stake that matters for most civic functions;
> the ballot alone waits for the oath (Lesson 32). Newcomers
> who engage early arrive at citizenship already fluent — the
> ceremony then admits someone who's been practicing.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* Name the three levels of government,
what each of your three representatives is called, and one
responsibility you'd contact each about.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Federal — Member of Parliament (MP): immigration files, EI, CRA,
passports, federal law. Provincial — MPP (Ontario) / MLA (most
provinces) / MNA (Quebec) / MHA (NL): health care, schools,
housing/tenancy law, driver's licences, employment standards.
Municipal — councillor (plus mayor and school trustee): zoning,
transit, roads, parks, local policing boards, permits. Routing
correctly is the first test of Canadian civic literacy
(Lesson 29's blame table, now as an action table).
</details>

**Q2.** What is constituency casework, who can use it, and when
should you?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
The service function of every MP/provincial member's riding
office: staff intervene with government departments on
constituents' stuck files — immigration and citizenship queues,
EI and CRA disputes, passports, benefits — through liaison
channels unavailable to the public. Open to all constituents,
citizens or not, regardless of how (or whether) they voted;
free; confidential. Use it when a file exceeds published
processing times or a bureaucratic error resists normal
channels: email the office with your file number, timeline, and
a one-paragraph summary. It is the single most underused
government service among newcomers — the people whose files
most often need it.
</details>

**Q3.** Give the participation menu available to a permanent
resident, and the two items that wait for citizenship.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Available now: contacting all representatives and casework;
deputations and public consultations; school councils and
municipal advisory committees; party membership in most parties
— including nomination and leadership votes; political
donations (individuals only — corporate/union money is banned);
volunteering, organizing, protesting (Charter-protected);
census participation (mandatory, in fact). Waiting for
citizenship: voting in elections (all three levels) and
standing as a candidate — plus jury duty, the compelled civic
service that arrives with the oath (Lessons 32–33). The
practical implication: civic life can begin on arrival day;
newcomers who use the menu reach citizenship already fluent.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-civic-culture"></a>**civic culture** — the habits of participation (voting, volunteering, showing up) that keep institutions honest.
- <a id="w-constituency-riding-office"></a>**constituency / riding office** — your representative's local office; <a id="w-casework"></a>**casework** — its service of unsticking constituents' files with the bureaucracy (this lesson's most valuable secret).
- <a id="w-mpp-mla-mna-mha"></a>**MPP / MLA / MNA / MHA** — what provincial representatives are called (Ontario / most provinces / Quebec / Newfoundland).
- <a id="w-ward"></a>**ward** — a city's electoral district; your **councillor** represents one.
- <a id="w-school-trustee"></a>**school trustee** — the elected member of a school board.
- <a id="w-deputation"></a>**deputation** (dep-yoo-TAY-shun) — formally addressing city council in person for a few minutes, on the public record.
- <a id="w-town-hall"></a>**town hall** — an open public meeting with a politician.
- <a id="w-nomination-contest"></a>**nomination contest** — the internal party vote choosing who becomes the party's candidate in a riding — often the real election in safe seats.
- <a id="w-petition"></a>**petition** — a signed group request; weaker than individual letters, per this lesson.
- <a id="w-bylaw"></a>**bylaw** (BY-law) — a municipal law (parking, noise, snow-shovelling).
- <a id="w-grassroots"></a>**grassroots** — driven by ordinary people rather than organizations.
- <a id="w-cvitp"></a>**CVITP** — the volunteer tax clinics that file returns free for low-income people (Lesson 35).
