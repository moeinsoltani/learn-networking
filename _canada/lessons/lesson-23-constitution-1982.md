# Lesson 23: The Constitution Comes Home

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **patriation** (pay-tree-AY-shun) — a made-in-Canada word: bringing the constitution "home" from Britain so Canada could amend it itself.
> - **entrenched** — locked into the constitution, beyond the reach of ordinary laws.
> - **constitutional convention** — an unwritten rule of politics that is binding in practice but not enforceable in court.
> - **unilateral** (yoo-nih-LAT-er-ul) — done by one party alone, without the others' agreement.
> - **fait accompli** (fet ah-kom-PLEE) — French: a thing already done, presented so it cannot be undone.
> - **veto** (VEE-toh) — the power to block a decision single-handedly.
> - **notwithstanding clause (s.33)** — "notwithstanding" = "despite"; the clause lets a legislature declare a law valid *despite* certain Charter rights, for renewable 5-year periods.
> - **parliamentary supremacy** — the older British principle that parliament's laws cannot be struck down by courts (the Charter ended this in Canada).
> - **accord** — a formal agreement between governments; **ratification** — the follow-up approvals that make it law.
> - **"distinct society"** — Meech Lake's proposed constitutional recognition of Quebec's uniqueness.
> - **MLA** — Member of the Legislative Assembly (a provincial parliament member); **unanimous consent** — agreement of every single member, letting a legislature skip normal delays.
> - Names: **Chrétien** (kray-tee-EN), **Meech Lake** (MEECH), **Bourassa** (boo-RASS-ah), **Elijah Harper** (ih-LY-jah), **Oji-Cree** (OH-jee-KREE), **Hull** (HULL — the Quebec city facing Ottawa, now Gatineau), **la nuit des longs couteaux** (lah NWEE day LON koo-TOH — "the night of the long knives").

## Concept

April 17, 1982, on a rainy Parliament Hill: the Queen signs, and
Canada's constitution finally lives in Canada — 115 years after
Confederation, 51 after the Statute of Westminster parked it in London
(Lesson 19). **Patriation** brought three things: an **amending
formula** (the problem that took the 51 years), the **Charter of
Rights and Freedoms** (Trudeau's monument, and since then the centre
of Canadian law and identity), and a **wound**: it was done **over
Quebec's objection**, after a final November 1981 night of dealmaking
Quebec calls *la nuit des longs couteaux* — the Night of the Long
Knives. Two subsequent attempts to fix that wound — **Meech Lake** and
**Charlottetown** — failed spectacularly, nearly breaking the country
(Lesson 22's 1995 referendum is Meech's direct child).

The sequence matters here more than in most lessons, because each step
constrains the next.

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| **1980** | Quebec's referendum returns a No, and Trudeau promises constitutional "renewal" in exchange (Lesson 22) |
| **1980–81** | Open federal–provincial war. Trudeau threatens to patriate the constitution unilaterally; eight provinces, Quebec among them, form the **"Gang of Eight"** against him |
| **September 1981** | The Supreme Court rules — very Canadianly — that unilateral patriation would be **legal but unconstitutional by convention** without substantial provincial consent |
| **November 4–5, 1981** | The **Kitchen Accord**, remembered in Quebec as *la nuit des longs couteaux* — the night of the long knives. Ottawa and nine provinces cut a deal in the small hours while Lévesque sleeps across the river in Hull. Quebec wakes to a done deal, and has never signed since |
| **April 17, 1982** | The **Constitution Act, 1982**: an amending formula at last, the **Charter of Rights and Freedoms** (including the section 33 override, which was the price of the deal), section 35 recognizing Indigenous rights, and a commitment to equalization |
| **1987–90** | The **Meech Lake Accord** — five conditions to bring Quebec in, "distinct society" among them — dies at its ratification deadline in Manitoba (Elijah Harper's feather) and Newfoundland |
| **1992** | The **Charlottetown Accord** tries to give everyone everything, and a national referendum kills it: 54.3% No |
| **1995** | Quebec's second referendum is a near-miss (Lesson 22) |
| **Since** | No government has touched mega-constitutional politics again |

The paradox to hold: the 1982 package is simultaneously Canada's
proudest legal achievement (the Charter consistently polls as the top
symbol of Canadian identity — *including in Quebec*) and its most
durable constitutional grievance (no Quebec government, federalist or
sovereignist, has ever signed). Both facts are load-bearing.

## Going Deeper

### The 1981 endgame

Trudeau, fresh from the referendum promise (Lesson 22), moved to
patriate with a charter, unilaterally if needed. Eight provinces
resisted (provincial rights, not rights-skepticism — mostly). The
Supreme Court's September 1981 Solomon ruling — *legal, but
conventionally improper without substantial consent* — forced one
last conference. On the night of November 4–5, federal justice
minister **Jean Chrétien** and provincial counterparts sketched the
compromise (the "Kitchen Accord"): Charter + amending formula, with
two prices — the **notwithstanding clause (s.33)**, letting
legislatures override core Charter sections for renewable 5-year
terms (the provinces' insurance against judge-rule), and dropping
Quebec's traditional **veto** (the amending formula went to
7-provinces/50%-population, with unanimity for a short list). The
nine anglophone premiers signed by morning; **Lévesque, lodged across
the river in Hull, was not in the room**. Quebec's telling:
betrayal-by-night among people who'd sworn common cause; the others'
telling: Lévesque had himself broken the Gang of Eight days earlier
by accepting Trudeau's referendum gambit, and a sovereignist premier
would never have signed *any* deal. Both tellings are taught; the
fact is the signature page.

### What 1982 actually contains

- **The Charter of Rights and Freedoms** — Lesson 31 tours it
  properly; here, its constitutional novelties: entrenched (no
  ordinary statute can override), judicially enforced (courts strike
  legislation — ending parliamentary supremacy in the British sense),
  but with two Canadian valves: **s.1** (rights subject to
  "reasonable limits demonstrably justified in a free and democratic
  society") and **s.33** (the override). Rights with adjustable
  pressure — the 1774/1848 accommodation habit (Lessons 10, 12),
  now applied to rights themselves.
- **s.35 — "existing aboriginal and treaty rights are hereby
  recognized and affirmed"**: outside the Charter (so s.33 can't
  touch it), inserted after Indigenous campaigning (including the
  "Constitution Express" train to Ottawa) forced its restoration
  when first drafts dropped it. Lesson 13's grammar repair
  (object → party) and the legal engine of Lessons 17/44's modern
  claims. Arguably 1982's sleeper — its most transformative clause.
- **The amending formula** — general rule 7/50; unanimity for the
  monarchy, the Court's composition, and the formula itself. Why
  mega-amendments are now near-impossible (see Meech).
- Equalization constitutionalized (s.36 — Lesson 35), minority-
  language education rights (s.23 — Lesson 42's francophone schools
  outside Quebec), and the renaming: BNA Act → Constitution Act,
  1867.

### Meech Lake (1987–90): the fix that broke worse

Mulroney (elected 1984 partly on reconciliation-with-Quebec) and
Quebec's federalist premier Bourassa negotiated **five conditions**
for Quebec's signature: recognition as a **"distinct society"**;
restored veto; immigration powers; Supreme Court appointments from
Quebec lists; spending-power limits. All ten premiers agreed —
then the 3-year ratification clock did its work: two provincial
elections changed governments; "distinct society" polled badly in
English Canada (two objections braided: Trudeau's — special status
betrays equal citizenship; and a popular one — why Quebec and not
us/Indigenous peoples/women?); and in the final week, in Manitoba's
legislature, **Elijah Harper** — Oji-Cree MLA — softly said "No,
Mr. Speaker," holding an eagle feather, denying the unanimous consent
needed to expedite: Indigenous peoples had been left out of Meech
entirely, *again*, and one man's procedural "no" spoke Lesson 13's
whole grammar lesson. Manitoba missed the deadline; Newfoundland
withdrew; Meech died June 1990. Consequences, immediate and huge:
Bouchard walked out of Mulroney's cabinet to found the **Bloc
Québécois**; Quebec sovereignty polling spiked past 60%; the road to
1995 was paved (Lesson 22).

### Charlottetown (1992): everything, therefore nothing

The repair-the-repair attempt: distinct society *plus* Senate reform
(the West's file) *plus* Indigenous **inherent right of
self-government** *plus* social charter *plus*... — negotiated by
everyone, endorsed by every premier, every major party, business,
labour. Put to a national referendum (October 1992): **No, 54.3%** —
rejected in Quebec (not enough), in the West (too much), and by
Charter-citizens everywhere (elites don't own the constitution —
Trudeau, in retirement, campaigned No and helped kill his
successors' deal). The lesson Canadian politics extracted, and has
obeyed since: **mega-constitutional politics loses**; change now
happens by ordinary statute, intergovernmental agreement, court
ruling, and quiet asymmetry (Lesson 22's actual equilibrium). The
constitution's text froze in 1982; the country kept moving around
it.

{: .note }
> **"Why does everything still work?"**
> A constitution rejected by a quarter of the federation, an
> override clause on its bill of rights, an amending formula too
> stiff to use — and yet: Quebec's civil servants apply the Charter
> daily; its courts enforce it; Quebec participates in amendments'
> ordinary politics; and the Charter polls beloved *in Quebec*. The
> resolution: Canada distinguishes **legal validity** (1982 binds
> Quebec — the Supreme Court confirmed; no signature required) from
> **political legitimacy** (the missing signature is a standing
> claim, banked for the next negotiation). The country runs on the
> gap — as it has since two parties signed different treaties on
> one paper (Lesson 17), which is either Canada's deepest flaw or
> its operating system. This course's verdict by now: both, and
> deliberately.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* What happened on April 17, 1982, and
what three major elements did the Constitution Act, 1982 add?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Queen Elizabeth II signed the Constitution Act, 1982 in Ottawa,
"patriating" the constitution — ending the British Parliament's
role and giving Canada full domestic control, 115 years after
Confederation. Additions: (1) the Canadian Charter of Rights and
Freedoms — entrenched, judicially enforced rights, with the s.1
reasonable-limits clause and s.33 notwithstanding override; (2) a
domestic amending formula (generally seven provinces with 50% of
population; unanimity for select matters); (3) s.35's recognition
and affirmation of existing Aboriginal and treaty rights (plus
s.36 equalization and s.23 minority-language education rights).
Quebec's government did not consent and has never formally signed
— though the Act legally binds it.
</details>

**Q2.** What were Meech Lake's five conditions, and name the two
distinct forces that killed it.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Conditions for Quebec's signature: recognition of Quebec as a
distinct society; a restored constitutional veto; increased
provincial power over immigration; Quebec's role in Supreme Court
appointments (from provincial lists); limits on federal spending
power in provincial jurisdiction. Killers: (1) English-Canadian
backlash against "distinct society" as special status (armed
intellectually by Trudeau's scorching interventions), which
flipped incoming provincial governments during the three-year
ratification window; (2) Indigenous exclusion — Elijah Harper's
procedural refusal in Manitoba's legislature (the eagle feather,
June 1990) ran out the clock, and Newfoundland then withdrew.
Aftermath: the Bloc Québécois founded, sovereignty support
soaring, the 1995 referendum — and a lasting national allergy to
constitutional mega-rounds (Charlottetown's 1992 referendum
defeat, 54.3% No, confirmed it).
</details>

**Q3.** Explain the legal-validity vs political-legitimacy
distinction that lets Canada function with Quebec unsigned — and
connect it to one earlier episode in the course.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Legal validity: the 1982 amendments met the then-applicable
requirements (substantial provincial consent; the Supreme Court
rejected Quebec's veto claim), so the constitution binds Quebec
fully — courts, governments, and daily administration proceed
accordingly. Political legitimacy: a constitution is also a
compact, and the missing consent of the province representing one
founding people is a real defect — formally acknowledged by all
parties, kept as Quebec's standing claim for future negotiation,
and honoured in practice through non-constitutional
accommodations (asymmetric arrangements, the 2006 "nation"
motion). Canada deliberately runs on the gap rather than forcing
its closure. Earlier echoes (any): the numbered treaties — legally
enforced texts whose legitimacy gap (two understandings, Lesson
17) is likewise managed rather than resolved; the "Dominion"
euphemism and unwritten conventions generally (Lessons 12–13);
or 1774's Quebec Act — binding law whose legitimacy was earned
after the fact by performance. The pattern is the course's spine:
Canadian stability lives less in settled documents than in
permanently managed disagreements.
</details>
