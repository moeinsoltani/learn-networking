# Lesson 22: Quebec — the Quiet Revolution to the Referendums

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

In 1960, Quebec was the most church-run society in the Western world —
education, hospitals, unions all **clerical**[°](#w-clerical); the highest birthrate;
francophones earning less on average than nearly every immigrant group
in their own province, managed in English above foreman level. Within
**one generation**: the most secular society in North America, a
state-building nationalism, a near-victorious independence movement,
and — twice — a referendum on leaving Canada, the second lost by
**54,288 votes**.

The arc runs sixty years, and it moves in one direction: from a nationalism of
*survival* to a nationalism of *statehood*, and then back to an uneasy truce.

| Years | What happened |
|---|---|
| **1936–59** | **Duplessis** — remembered in Quebec as *la Grande Noirceur*, "the Great Darkness": Church authority, **patronage**[°](#w-patronage), and resources sold cheap to outside investors. Nationalism here means *survival* — rural, Catholic, defensive (the *la survivance* of Lessons 08 and 10) |
| **1960** | The **Lesage Liberals** win on the slogan *"Il faut que ça change"* — and the **Quiet Revolution** begins. The state takes over education and health from the Church; Hydro-Québec is nationalized under the banner *"Maîtres chez nous"* (masters in our own house); and a francophone managerial class is deliberately built through the public sector |
| **1963–70** | **FLQ** bombings — the violent fringe of the movement |
| **1967** | De Gaulle's balcony speech (Lesson 21); **René Lévesque** leaves the Liberals and founds the **Parti Québécois** in 1968 |
| **1970** | The **October Crisis**: the FLQ kidnaps James Cross and Pierre Laporte, and murders Laporte; Trudeau invokes the **War Measures Act** — troops in Montreal, more than 400 detained without charge |
| **1976–77** | The **PQ wins power**; the next year brings **Bill 101**, the Charter of the French Language |
| **1980** | **First referendum** on **sovereignty-association**[°](#w-sovereignty-association): **No 59.6%, Yes 40.4%** — Trudeau promises constitutional renewal |
| **1982** | Patriation of the constitution **without Quebec's signature** (Lesson 23) |
| **1990–92** | The **Meech Lake Accord** dies; the **Charlottetown Accord** is rejected |
| **1995** | **Second referendum**: **No 50.58%, Yes 49.42%** |
| **2000s–** | **Sovereignty**[°](#w-sovereignty) dormant but not dead; Parliament recognizes the Québécois as "a nation within a united Canada" (2006) |

Two men, schoolmates-in-spirit and total opposites, personify the era:
**René Lévesque** (chain-smoking journalist, the Quiet Revolution
minister who nationalized hydro, founder of the PQ — sovereignty by
ballot, gradual, democratic) and **Pierre Trudeau** (intellectual,
anti-nationalist, federal Justice Minister then PM — French power *in
Ottawa*, bilingual federalism, one Canada with entrenched rights). The
entire period is their argument, and Canada is still furnished with
both men's answers.

## Going Deeper

### The Quiet Revolution: the state replaces the Church

Premier **Jean Lesage** (1960–66), slogan *"Maîtres chez nous"*:
education secularized and exploded (Ministry of Education 1964 —
previously none; CEGEPs; Université du Québec network); hospitals and
social services taken from religious orders; the civil service
professionalized and made a francophone career ladder; and the
masterstroke — **René Lévesque's 1962–63 nationalization of the
private hydro companies into Hydro-Québec** (Lesson 05), which became
simultaneously a world-class utility, an engineering school for a
francophone managerial class that had been shut out of anglo corporate
Canada, and the *proof of concept*: the Québécois state could run big
things in French. Vocations collapsed in step — Sunday practice went
from ~85% to Europe's lows within two decades; the Church that had
held *la survivance* together since 1763 (Lessons 08, 10) emptied in
one generation. Nationalism changed vector accordingly: from
*survivance* (defensive, Catholic, rural — survive as a people) to
*épanouissement* (state-powered, secular, urban — flourish as a
nation, with a state to do it).

### The violent fringe and the October Crisis

The **FLQ** (Front de libération du Québec) — a few dozen
Marxist-decolonization cells — bombed mailboxes, armouries, and the
Montreal Stock Exchange (1963–70; ~8 deaths). October 1970: cells
kidnapped British trade commissioner **James Cross**, then Quebec
labour minister **Pierre Laporte**. Trudeau — asked how far he'd go:
**"Just watch me"** — invoked the **War Measures Act** (Lesson 20's
loaded statute; its only peacetime use): troops on Montreal streets,
civil liberties suspended, ~450 detained without charge (nearly all
released uncharged — poets, singers, the sovereignist **intelligentsia**[°](#w-intelligentsia)).
Laporte was found strangled in a car trunk; Cross was released
negotiated-exile-for-hostage in December. Verdicts still split: firm
hand that ended terrorism (the FLQ evaporated; support in Quebec at
the time was overwhelming) vs. massive overreach against a movement
police work could have handled (the detentions hit the *democratic*
independence movement, not the cells). Two things are certain: the
Emergencies Act (1988) exists because of it, and **Lévesque's ballot
route won the argument inside the movement** — after 1970, Quebec
nationalism is electoral, full stop.

### Bill 101: the language state

The PQ's first year (1977) produced the **Charter of the French
Language** — French the sole official language of Quebec; mandatory
French schooling for immigrants' children (the anglo school system had
been assimilating newcomers into English — Montreal demographics were
the detonator); French commercial signage (later softened to "marked
predominance" after Charter litigation — Lesson 31's s.33 story);
francization of workplaces. Results, honestly stated: it *worked* —
French is secure in Montreal in a way 1970 doubted, immigrants
integrate in French, and the anglo exodus it accelerated (~200,000,
plus head offices down the 401 to Toronto — which is partly *why*
Toronto overtook Montreal, Lesson 04) permanently changed both
cities. It remains the most loved and most litigated statute in
Quebec — loved *as* the guarantee la survivance no longer had a
Church to provide; litigated because its collective-rights logic
collides with the Charter's individual-rights logic — the exact
collision Lesson 23 constitutionalizes.

### Two referendums

- **1980 — "sovereignty-association":** the PQ's soft question
  (negotiate sovereignty *with* economic association, second
  referendum to ratify). Trudeau's counter: vote No and "we will
  renew the federation" (the promise whose 1982 fulfillment Quebec
  read as betrayal — Lesson 23). **No, 59.6%.** Lévesque's concession:
  *"À la prochaine fois"* — until next time.
- **1995 — the near-death:** post-Meech fury (Lesson 23), Lucien
  Bouchard's charisma; question again association-flavoured
  ("sovereignty after a formal offer of partnership"). Turnout
  **93.5%**. **No, 50.58–49.42** — 54,288 votes. Premier Parizeau's
  concession-night line — blaming "money and ethnic votes" — ended
  his career overnight and handed federalists a decade of moral
  ammunition. Aftermath: the **Clarity Act (2000)** (secession
  requires a *clear question* and *clear majority*, per the Supreme
  Court's Secession Reference — which also held Canada would be
  obliged to negotiate in good faith with a clear Yes); and the 2006
  Commons motion recognizing "the **Québécois form a nation within a
  united Canada**" — symbolic, deliberately ambiguous, very Canadian.

Sovereignty since: dormant, not dead — support cycles 30–40%,
generational replacement cuts both ways, and the movement's energy
migrated into language/identity legislation (Bill 96, **laïcité**[°](#w-laicite) —
Lesson 43's debates). The question Lesson 47 inherits: can a country
permanently contain a nation that reserves the right to leave?
Canada's answer to date: apparently yes, by never making it answer.

{: .note }
> **How to talk about Quebec — the operating manual**
> (1) "Québécois" ≠ "French Canadian": the first is the modern
> national-civic identity (anyone in the Quebec project); the second
> is the pre-1960 ethnic-religious category (and includes Acadians,
> Franco-Ontarians etc., who are *not* Québécois — Lesson 42).
> (2) Sovereignty support was never hatred of Canada — polls
> consistently showed Yes voters wanting to keep the dollar,
> passport-adjacent ties, even the Canadiens; it was about being a
> majority somewhere rather than a permanent 2% of North America.
> (3) Never say "the separatists lost, it's over" in Montreal — and
> never say "Quebec's demands are endless" without knowing Meech
> (Lesson 23). Both are conversation-enders in opposite rooms.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* What was the Quiet Revolution, and what
does "Maîtres chez nous" refer to?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Quebec's rapid 1960s transformation under Jean Lesage's Liberals:
the provincial state took over education, health, and social
services from the Catholic Church, modernized and secularized
society, and built a francophone professional and managerial class
through state institutions. "Maîtres chez nous" — "masters in our
own house" — was the 1962 slogan, embodied in René Lévesque's
nationalization of private electricity companies into Hydro-Québec:
proof that Québécois could own and run their economy in French. The
era transformed nationalism from Church-centred survival to
state-centred assertion — the soil of everything from Bill 101 to
the referendums.
</details>

**Q2.** What happened in October 1970, what statute was invoked, and
what are the two standing verdicts on that invocation?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
The FLQ kidnapped British diplomat James Cross and Quebec minister
Pierre Laporte; Laporte was murdered. Trudeau's government — "Just
watch me" — invoked the War Measures Act, its only peacetime use:
troops deployed, ~450 arrested without charge (nearly all never
charged), civil liberties suspended; Cross was freed in December in
exchange for the kidnappers' exile. Verdicts: (a) decisive action
that ended a terror campaign with broad public support at the time,
including in Quebec; (b) grave overreach — the detentions swept up
the lawful independence movement, police already had the leads
needed, and the precedent chilled dissent. Legislated consequence:
the Emergencies Act (1988) replaced the War Measures Act with
parliamentary review and Charter compliance. Political consequence:
Quebec nationalism thereafter ran exclusively through the ballot —
Lévesque's route.
</details>

**Q3.** Give the dates, questions (in substance), and results of both
referendums, plus one major consequence of each.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
1980: sovereignty-association — a mandate to negotiate sovereignty
with economic association, ratified by a second vote. No 59.6% /
Yes 40.4%. Consequence: Trudeau's renewal promise led to the 1982
patriation — achieved without Quebec's signature, the grievance that
dominated the next decade (Lesson 23). 1995: sovereignty after a
formal partnership offer. No 50.58% / Yes 49.42% on 93.5% turnout —
a 54,288-vote margin. Consequences: the Clarity Act (2000, following
the Supreme Court's Secession Reference: clear question + clear
majority required, negotiation obligatory on a clear Yes);
Parizeau's "money and ethnic votes" remark discrediting the
movement's civic image; and a long sovereignty winter — support
cycling in the 30s, the question unresolved but unasked since.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-secular-secularize"></a>**secular / secularize** (SEK-yoo-ler) — not religious / to move something (schools, hospitals) out of religious control; <a id="w-clerical"></a>**clerical** — run by the Church.
- <a id="w-patronage"></a>**patronage** (PAT-run-ij) — handing out government jobs and contracts to political friends.
- <a id="w-nationalize"></a>**nationalize** — for the state to buy or take over private companies (here: the electricity companies → Hydro-Québec).
- <a id="w-sovereignty"></a>**sovereignty** (SOV-rin-tee) — here: Quebec becoming an independent country; <a id="w-sovereignty-association"></a>**sovereignty-association** — independence *plus* a negotiated economic partnership with Canada.
- <a id="w-anglophone-francophone-allophone"></a>**anglophone / francophone / allophone** (AN-gloh-fone / FRAN-koh-fone / AL-oh-fone) — English-speaker / French-speaker / (in Quebec) someone whose first language is neither.
- <a id="w-cegep"></a>**CEGEP** (say-ZHEP) — Quebec's free college level between high school and university.
- <a id="w-laicite"></a>**laïcité** (lah-ee-see-TAY) — French-style strict separation of religion from the state.
- <a id="w-intelligentsia"></a>**intelligentsia** (in-tel-ih-GENT-see-ah) — a society's intellectual class (writers, artists, academics).
- Names & phrases: **Duplessis** (doo-pless-EE), **la Grande Noirceur** (lah grawnd nwahr-SUR — "the Great Darkness"), **Lesage** (luh-SAHZH), **Lévesque** (lay-VEK), **Trudeau** (troo-DOH), **Parti Québécois / PQ** (par-TEE kay-bay-KWAH), **FLQ** (say the letters; in French: ef-el-KEW), **Bouchard** (boo-SHAR), **Parizeau** (pah-ree-ZOH), **"Maîtres chez nous"** (METR shay NOO — "masters in our own house"), **"À la prochaine fois"** (ah lah proh-SHEN FWAH — "until next time"), **épanouissement** (ay-pah-noo-ees-MAWN — "flourishing").
