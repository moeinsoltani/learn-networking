# Lesson 42: Two Languages

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

Start with the correction that defuses most confusion: **official
bilingualism does not mean Canadians are bilingual, or must be.** It
means the **federal state** operates in both English and French —
your cereal box, your tax forms, your court hearing, your air-traffic
control, on demand, in either. Individuals owe nothing; the
*government* owes everything twice.

Start with who is officially what, because it is less uniform than people
assume. **Canada as a country is officially bilingual**, under the Official
Languages Act of 1969 — the product of Pearson's Royal Commission on
Bilingualism and Biculturalism, legislated under Trudeau, renewed in 1988 and
modernized in 2023. **Quebec is officially French**, under Bill 101
(Lesson 22). **New Brunswick is the only officially bilingual province**, and
constitutionally so — the Acadians of Lesson 08 secured that. The other twelve
provinces and territories are English in practice, with French services
provided by policy or statute in patches, as under Ontario's French Language
Services Act.

Then the human numbers, which tell a more interesting story than the legal
map:

| Measure | Roughly |
|---|---|
| Mother-tongue French speakers | ~21% of Canadians, and falling as a share |
| French–English bilinguals | ~18% nationally — but **~45% inside Quebec** versus 7–12% outside it |
| Francophones outside Quebec | About one million: Acadians (~30% of New Brunswick), Franco-Ontarians (500–600,000), St. Boniface in Winnipeg, and pockets everywhere |
| Anglophones inside Quebec | About one million, concentrated in Montreal |

Look hard at the bilingualism row, because it carries the lesson's real point:
**bilingualism in Canada is mostly a francophone skill.** The burden of
learning the other language falls overwhelmingly on French speakers, and a
great deal of the politics in this lesson follows from that asymmetry.

The design's logic, from the whole course: 1774's bargain
(Lesson 10) → Confederation's s.133 (Lesson 13) → the Quiet
Revolution's pressure (Lesson 22) → the Official Languages Act as
federal Canada's answer to the question "can a francophone live a
full life in this country's institutions?" The answer's scorecard —
genuinely mixed — is this lesson's honest cargo.

## Going Deeper

### What the Official Languages Act actually requires

Federal services in both languages **where numbers warrant**
(offices, courts, Service Canada, Crown corporations); both
languages equal in Parliament and federal courts; the right to
**work** in either language inside designated federal institutions;
bilingual packaging (the cereal box — actually the Consumer
Packaging Act, same spirit); official-language **minority
education** rights constitutionalized in **Charter s.23**
(Lesson 31): francophone schools outside Quebec and anglophone
schools within it, where numbers warrant — the clause that rebuilt
Franco-Ontarian and Acadian school systems and the one Charter
right Quebec governments use *against* extension (the "where
numbers warrant" litigation file is permanent). What it never
required: any individual to learn anything, any province (except
constitutionalized NB) to change anything, any private business
outside federal jurisdiction to serve anyone in anything.

### The francophonies outside Quebec (say their names)

- **Acadians** (L.08's survivors): ~30% of New Brunswick —
  their weight made NB officially bilingual (1969,
  constitutionalized 1982/1993); the Acadian flag (tricolour
  with gold star), August 15, the *tintamarre*[°](#w-tintamarre) — a distinct
  people, emphatically not Québécois.
- **Franco-Ontarians**: half a million-plus; their 1912–27
  Regulation 17 suppression (Lesson 18's conscription-era
  grievance) and its long repair (French-language schools,
  courts, the 1975 flag, the 2018 university fight) form
  Ontario's own miniature language history.
- **St. Boniface / Franco-Manitobans**: Riel's community
  (Lesson 15) — Manitoba entered Confederation officially
  bilingual (Manitoba Act, 1870), abolished French illegally in
  1890, and had a century of laws invalidated by the Supreme
  Court in 1985 when the bill came due: the course's tidiest
  proof that constitutional promises don't expire (Lesson 15, provincial edition).
- Plus Franco-Albertans, Fransaskois, Franco-Ténois... each
  with flag, festival, and school board — the "French fact"
  is national, thin, and tenacious.

### French immersion: the anglophone institution

The unpredicted social phenomenon: **French immersion**[°](#w-french-immersion) —
anglophone children schooled substantially in French — became
English Canada's most oversubscribed public-education stream
(waitlists, lotteries, 5 a.m. registration queues). Honest
double reading: genuine bilingual-idealism *and* **de facto**[°](#w-de-facto)
streaming (immersion as the free private school — the
middle-class sorting mechanism its critics document). Output:
hundreds of thousands of functionally bilingual anglophone
graduates — and the political class's bilingualism requirement
(no unilingual PM since the 1960s is electorally viable;
Lesson 27) now supplied largely by immersion alumni.

### The honest scorecard

**Successes:** a francophone can deal with the federal state,
serve in cabinet, command the army, and become PM entirely in
French — the 1960s' impossible dream, now unremarkable
(Lesson 22's Trudeau column, verified); s.23 rebuilt minority
schooling; NB works; the courts enforce (Manitoba 1985,
Montfort Hospital, school-numbers cases). **Failures/limits:**
bilingualism outside Quebec and NB plateaued near 10% —
coast-to-coast personal bilingualism never happened (Lévesque's
column, verified: the *state* is bilingual; the *society* is
two unilingualisms with a bilingual seam); French's national
share erodes with each census (immigration arithmetic — hence
Quebec's francophone-immigration selection, L.22/41, and the
2023 Act's new asymmetric tilt toward protecting French
specifically, a quiet doctrinal shift: from symmetric
bilingualism to defending the minority language); and the
program's costs/irritants feed a perennial western "why am I
paying for this?" grumble (Lesson 35's routing: because 1774 →
1867 → 1969 is the price of the country existing — the course's
answer, available to you now).

{: .note }
> **Working vocabulary (deploy with care)**
> *Francophone/anglophone/allophone* — mother-tongue French /
> English / other (the census trinity; "allophone" is normal
> Canadian English). *Québécois* ≠ *French Canadian* ≠
> *francophone* (L.22's manual — nation vs ethnicity vs
> language). *Franglais*[°](#w-franglais) — the Montreal register-switch;
> *joual*[°](#w-joual) — Quebec working-class French, reclaimed by its
> literature. And the phrase to retire: "Parisian French vs
> Quebec French" as better/worse — Quebec French is a
> 400-year-old variety (L.08's founder effect) with its own
> standard; calling it broken French in Montreal is the
> linguistic equivalent of calling American English broken
> British. It goes poorly.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* What does official bilingualism
require, of whom — and which is the only officially bilingual
province?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
It requires the FEDERAL state — Parliament, courts, services
where numbers warrant, federal workplaces, packaging — to
operate in English and French; it imposes nothing on
individuals and (almost) nothing on provinces. New Brunswick
is the only officially bilingual province (the Acadian
fact, constitutionally entrenched); Quebec is officially
French (Bill 101); the rest are English-operating with
varying French-services statutes. Charter s.23 adds
minority-language education rights on both sides of the
divide, where numbers warrant.
</details>

**Q2.** Give the bilingualism scorecard: two verified successes,
two honest limits, with the Trudeau/Lévesque columns from
Lesson 22 settled.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Successes: the federal state genuinely works in French — a
francophone can be served, employed, judged, and elected PM
in French (Trudeau's column verified: French power in Ottawa
became routine); minority education rebuilt — s.23 schools
restored Franco-Ontarian and Acadian systems, with courts
enforcing (Manitoba 1985, Montfort). Limits: personal
bilingualism outside Quebec/NB plateaued near 7–12% — the
bilingual coast-to-coast society never materialized
(Lévesque's column verified: French's real security came
from Quebec's own state power — Bill 101 — not federal
symmetry); and French's national share falls each census,
pushing policy from symmetric bilingualism toward asymmetric
protection of French (the 2023 Act). Settled verdict, per
Lesson 22's lab: Trudeau won the law and the state;
Lévesque won the sociology; Canada runs both men's answers
simultaneously.
</details>

**Q3.** Why is the Manitoba 1890–1985 story the course's
cleanest proof about constitutional promises?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Manitoba entered Confederation (Manitoba Act, 1870 — Riel's
negotiated terms, Lesson 15) constitutionally bilingual; in
1890 the province simply legislated French away — schools
and official status — and governed unilingually for
ninety-five years. In the 1985 Manitoba Language Rights
reference, the Supreme Court held the 1870 guarantee had
never stopped binding: nearly a century of unilingual
statutes was invalid (temporarily preserved to avoid chaos,
then re-enacted bilingually). Proof delivered: constitutional
promises to minorities don't lapse through violation,
majority preference, or time — the same doctrine carrying
the treaties (Lesson 17's live documents) and the MMF
ruling (Lesson 15): in Canadian law, the bill always eventually
arrives, and the Riel-era promises produced two of its
canonical deliveries.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-official-bilingualism"></a>**official bilingualism** — the *government* must operate in both languages; individuals owe nothing (the lesson's core correction).
- <a id="w-mother-tongue"></a>**mother tongue** — the first language a person learned and still understands (the census measure).
- <a id="w-where-numbers-warrant"></a>**"where numbers warrant"** — the legal threshold: services/schools required only where enough speakers live.
- <a id="w-de-facto"></a>**de facto** (deh FAK-toh) — true in practice, though not written in law (most provinces are de facto English).
- <a id="w-french-immersion"></a>**French immersion** — schooling anglophone children largely *in* French.
- <a id="w-symmetric-vs-asymmetric"></a>**symmetric vs asymmetric** — treating both languages identically vs deliberately protecting the weaker one (French).
- <a id="w-b-b-commission"></a>**B&B Commission** — the 1960s Royal Commission on Bilingualism and Biculturalism, parent of the 1969 Act.
- <a id="w-joual"></a>**joual** (zhoo-AL) — Quebec working-class French; <a id="w-franglais"></a>**franglais** (frawn-GLEH) — French-English mixing.
- <a id="w-tintamarre"></a>**tintamarre** (tan-tah-MAR) — the Acadian tradition of a deafening pot-banging parade on August 15.
- Names: **Acadians** (ah-KAY-dee-unz), **Franco-Ontarians**, **Fransaskois** (frawn-sass-KWAH — Saskatchewan's francophones), **St. Boniface** (BON-ih-fass), **Montfort** (mon-FOR — the Ottawa francophone hospital saved by court fight).
