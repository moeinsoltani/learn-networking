# Lesson 19: Between the Wars

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **general strike** — when workers across *all* industries in a place stop work at once; **collective bargaining** — workers negotiating pay as a united group through a union.
> - **Bolshevism** (BOHL-sheh-vizm) — Russian-revolution communism; in 1919 the establishment's all-purpose scare word.
> - **sedition / seditious libel** (sih-DISH-un) — the crime of speech that supposedly stirs rebellion against the state.
> - **magistrate** (MAJ-iss-trate) — a judge in a lower court.
> - **reference case** — a government asking the courts a legal question directly, without a normal lawsuit.
> - **Privy Council** (PRIV-ee) — here: the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London, then the Empire's highest court of appeal.
> - **"living tree" doctrine** — the rule that a constitution grows with the times instead of staying frozen at its writing.
> - **eugenics** (yoo-JEN-iks) — the discredited movement to "improve" the population by controlling who could have children; **sterilization** — surgery making someone unable to reproduce.
> - **amending formula** — the agreed rules for *who must consent* before a constitution can be changed.
> - **relief** — 1930s word for welfare payments; **relief camps** — work camps for unemployed single men.
> - **drought** (DROWT) — a long period without rain; the **Palliser Triangle** (PAL-ih-ser) — the dry prairie region hit hardest.
> - **manifesto** (man-ih-FESS-toh) — a public declaration of a movement's beliefs and plans.
> - Names: **Woodsworth** (WOODZ-worth), **McClung** (muh-KLUNG), **Sankey** (SANK-ee), **Aberhart** (AB-er-hart), **Rowell–Sirois** (ROH-el seer-WAH), **CCF** (say the letters) — the socialist party that became today's **NDP**.

## Concept

Twenty-one years separate the armistices, and in them Canada: won
legislative independence (**Statute of Westminster, 1931**), had its
constitutional definition of "person" corrected to include women (the
**Persons Case, 1929**), staged its most famous labour confrontation
(**Winnipeg General Strike, 1919**), and was hit harder by the **Great
Depression** than almost any developed country — an experience that
created the political parties and policy instincts (including the seed of
medicare) that still structure Canadian politics.

Four things happen in these twenty years, and each one winds a spring that
releases later in the century.

| Event | What it set in motion |
|---|---|
| **1919 — Winnipeg General Strike** | Labour politics as a permanent force, a lasting "foreign agitator" panic, and the career of J.S. Woodsworth |
| **1929 — the Persons Case** | Women legally "persons," and therefore eligible for the Senate; and the "living tree" doctrine that lets the constitution grow |
| **1931 — Statute of Westminster** | Legislative independence from Britain — with the constitution itself left in London "for safekeeping" (Lesson 23) |
| **1929–39 — the Depression** | A Prairie catastrophe of drought and collapsed wheat prices; relief camps and the On-to-Ottawa Trek (1935); brand-new parties — the CCF, ancestor of the NDP and of medicare, and Social Credit; the Bank of Canada (1935); and the Rowell–Sirois inquiry, whose logic becomes equalization |

The mental model: the interwar years are **Canada's independence and its
welfare state both being conceived** — the first by statute, the second
by suffering.

## Going Deeper

### Winnipeg, 1919: six weeks that named the sides

May–June 1919: ~30,000 workers — nearly the whole working city, from
metal-trades men to telephone operators ("Hello Girls") — walked out
over wages, inflation (prices had doubled in the war), and the right to
bargain collectively. The city split into strike committee (which kept
milk and bread moving) and the establishment's Citizens' Committee,
which read Bolshevism into everything (Winnipeg's strike leaders were
mostly British-born social-gospel types; the "foreign Bolshevik" framing
targeted, once again, Lesson 16's East Europeans). Ottawa sided with
capital: Mounties charged a crowd on **Bloody Saturday** (June 21 — two
dead, the famous overturned streetcar photo), leaders were arrested,
the strike collapsed. Long game, though: strike leader **J.S.
Woodsworth**, jailed for seditious libel (charges included quoting
Isaiah), was elected MP in 1921 and in 1932 founded the **CCF** — the
party that becomes the NDP and delivers medicare (Lesson 21). Canadian
labour history's arc in one city: lose the street, win the ballot,
change the country.

### The Persons Case, 1929: "persons" includes women

Section 24 of the BNA Act let the GG summon "qualified **persons**" to
the Senate; the government's lawyers held this excluded women (common-law
precedent: women were persons "in matters of pains and penalties, not
rights and privileges"). Alberta's **Famous Five** — **Emily Murphy**
(the Empire's first woman magistrate, whose appointment had been
challenged on exactly this ground), **Nellie McClung** (Lesson 18's
suffrage general), Henrietta Muir Edwards, Louise McKinney, Irene
Parlby — forced a reference case. The Supreme Court said no (1928);
the Empire's real top court, the **Judicial Committee of the Privy
Council**, said yes (1929), in the most-quoted sentence of Canadian
constitutional law — Lord Sankey: the exclusion of women was "a relic
of days more barbarous than ours," and the constitution is **"a living
tree capable of growth and expansion within its natural limits."**

Two legacies: women in the Senate (Cairine Wilson, 1930 — though not
Murphy: too troublesome), and the **living tree doctrine** — Canada's
official interpretive philosophy, the reason the Charter (Lesson 31)
is read expansively rather than by founders' intent. October 18 is
Persons Day. Required honesty: several of the Five, Murphy especially,
were also eugenics advocates (Alberta's Sexual Sterilization Act,
1928–72, disproportionately hit Indigenous and disabled women) —
Canadian commemoration now standardly carries both facts, and so
should you.

### 1931: independence by statute

The **Statute of Westminster** (following the 1926 Balfour Declaration
— dominions as "autonomous Communities... equal in status," itself
partly precipitated by the 1926 **King–Byng affair**, parked here for
Lesson 25) made Canada's Parliament fully sovereign: no British law
could apply to Canada without consent; Canada gained full treaty and
foreign-policy capacity (first legation: Washington, 1927 — Vincent
Massey). The one carve-out, at *Canada's own request*: amendment of
the BNA Act stayed in Britain — federal and provincial governments
couldn't agree on an amending formula, so the constitution stayed
"in a British safety-deposit box" for **51 more years**. Remember the
reason (domestic deadlock, not imperial refusal): it is the entire
setup for Lesson 23.

### The Depression: catastrophe as constitution-maker

Canada's export-staples economy (Lesson 05) made it the developed
world's second-hardest-hit country: GDP down ~40%, unemployment near
30%, wheat prices down two-thirds — and on the Prairies the market
collapse coincided with **the drought**: eight years of dust storms,
grasshoppers, and crop failure in the Palliser Triangle; Saskatchewan's
per-capita income fell ~70% and the province that immigration built
(Lesson 16) began emptying. Single unemployed men were warehoused in
military-run **relief camps** (20¢/day); in 1935 more than a thousand
rode boxcars east in the **On-to-Ottawa Trek**, stopped in Regina by
RCMP on Dominion Day — the **Regina Riot**, one constable dead,
hundreds injured.

What the misery built:

- **New parties, Prairie-made:** the **CCF** (Regina Manifesto, 1933:
  "No CCF Government will rest content until it has eradicated
  capitalism") — parliamentary socialism, farm-labour alliance,
  Woodsworth presiding; and **Social Credit** (Alberta 1935, "Bible
  Bill" Aberhart's monetary miracle cure) — Prairie protest's
  right-hand version. Canada's multi-party system — the thing that
  distinguishes its politics from the U.S. two-party pattern
  (Lesson 28) — is a Depression artifact.
- **New machinery:** the **Bank of Canada** (1935 — Lesson 40's
  protagonist); the CBC (1936 — Lesson 46); Trans-Canada Air Lines
  (1937); unemployment insurance (1940, by constitutional amendment —
  the provinces held the power, Lesson 29's lists biting hard); and
  the **Rowell–Sirois Commission** (1937–40), whose diagnosis — 
  provinces carry welfare burdens their tax bases can't fund;
  therefore the federation must transfer — is the intellectual birth
  certificate of **equalization** (Lesson 35).
- **A governing instinct:** "never again" — the Depression discredited
  pure-market orthodoxy for two generations of Canadian policy, and
  its memory is the emotional floor under every program in Lesson 34.

{: .note }
> **R.B. Bennett, the Depression's unlucky PM**
> Conservative PM 1930–35, a millionaire presiding over breadlines;
> "Bennett buggies" were cars pulled by horses (no money for gas). His
> deathbed-of-the-mandate conversion — a 1935 "Bennett New Deal" radio
> series proposing minimum wages and insurance — was too late
> politically and mostly struck down constitutionally (s.92 again). He
> retired to England, the only PM buried outside Canada. Mackenzie
> King inherited the file — and King's wartime-and-after governments
> built much of what Bennett sketched. Canadian pattern worth
> flagging: ideas often enter with the loser and get implemented by
> the winner.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* What did the Statute of Westminster
(1931) change, and what one thing did it deliberately leave in
Britain — and why?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
It made Canada (and the other dominions) legislatively independent:
no British statute would extend to Canada without Canadian consent,
and Canada gained full authority over its own laws and foreign
affairs — completing the autonomy the war and the 1926 Balfour
Declaration had established politically. Left behind, at Canada's own
request: the power to amend the BNA Act, because Ottawa and the
provinces could not agree on a domestic amending formula. Custody
lasted until patriation in 1982 (Lesson 23).
</details>

**Q2.** What was the Persons Case — parties, question, both courts'
answers, and the doctrine it left behind?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
The Famous Five (Emily Murphy, Nellie McClung, Henrietta Muir
Edwards, Louise McKinney, Irene Parlby) forced a reference on whether
"qualified persons" in s.24 of the BNA Act — Senate eligibility —
included women. Supreme Court of Canada, 1928: no (original intent).
Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, 1929: yes — Lord Sankey
ruling the exclusion "a relic of days more barbarous than ours" and
declaring the constitution "a living tree capable of growth and
expansion." Legacy: women became summonable to the Senate (Cairine
Wilson, 1930), and the living-tree doctrine became the master rule of
Canadian constitutional interpretation. Commemorated as Persons Day
(October 18); remembered today alongside the honest footnote of
several of the Five's eugenics advocacy.
</details>

**Q3.** Trace the chain: Depression → new Prairie parties → a lasting
feature of Canadian politics and a lasting program.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
The Depression hit the wheat economy hardest (staples exposure +
drought), and the Prairies answered with new parties: the CCF
(1932–33, Woodsworth — social-gospel socialism out of the Winnipeg
strike's legacy) and Social Credit (1935, Aberhart's Alberta).
Lasting feature: a genuine multi-party system — Canada's Parliament
has carried three-to-five parties ever since, making minority
governments and regional parties normal (Lesson 28). Lasting program:
the CCF's Saskatchewan government under Tommy Douglas (from 1944)
piloted public hospital insurance, then medicare — nationalized in
the 1960s (Lesson 21) — making Canada's signature social program a
direct Depression descendant. (Also creditable: the Bank of Canada,
unemployment insurance, and the Rowell–Sirois logic that became
equalization.)
</details>
