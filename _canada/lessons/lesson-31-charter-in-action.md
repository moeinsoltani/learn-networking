# Lesson 31: The Charter in Action

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **infringement** (in-FRINJ-ment) — a limiting or violation of a right.
> - **the Oakes test** (OAKS) — the court checklist deciding whether a rights limit is justified (explained below).
> - **proportionality** — the requirement that a law's harm to rights be in proportion to its benefit; **minimal impairment** — limiting the right as little as reasonably possible.
> - **analogous grounds** (ah-NAL-oh-gus) — new categories of discrimination courts may add to s.15's list (sexual orientation was added this way).
> - **override** — s.33's power to make a law operate despite certain rights; a **sunset clause** — an automatic expiry date (s.33's five years).
> - **remedy** — what a court orders to fix a rights violation; **strike down** — declare a law invalid; **suspended declaration** — striking a law but delaying the effect so Parliament can redraft.
> - **pre-emptive** — done in advance, before courts can rule.
> - **chilling effect** — when fear of punishment silences even legal speech.
> - **expropriation** (eks-proh-pree-AY-shun) — the government taking private property (with compensation, by statute — not a Charter right in Canada).
> - **"dialogue"** — the Canadian idea that courts and legislatures answer each other (ruling → redraft → re-ruling) rather than one having the final word.
> - Names: **Morgentaler** (MOR-gen-tah-ler), **Keegstra** (KEEG-strah), **Oakes** (OAKS), **Bedford**, **Carter** — landmark cases are known by the parties' names.

## Concept

Lesson 23 gave you the Charter's birth; this lesson gives you its
operating manual. The **Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms**
(1982) is the country's supreme rights law — and its design differs
from the American model in one philosophical sentence: **Canadian
rights are strong presumptions inside a balancing system, not
absolutes.** The system has exactly three moving parts:

The Charter is best understood as a machine with three moving parts: the
rights themselves, a balancer that lets the state limit them, and an override
that lets legislatures switch some of them off.

**The rights** run from section 2 to section 23:

| Sections | What they protect |
|---|---|
| **s.2** | Fundamental freedoms: conscience and religion, expression, peaceful assembly, association |
| **ss.3–5** | Democratic rights — the right to vote, and elections at least every five years |
| **s.6** | Mobility — the right to live and work anywhere in Canada |
| **ss.7–14** | Legal rights: life, liberty and security of the person (s.7); protection from unreasonable search and seizure (s.8); from arbitrary detention (s.9); the right to counsel (s.10); the presumption of innocence (s.11) |
| **s.15** | Equality — including *analogous* grounds that courts may add to the listed ones. Sexual orientation entered Canadian law exactly this way |
| **ss.16–23** | Language rights: official bilingualism, and minority-language education (Lesson 42) |

**The balancer is section 1.** Every right in the Charter is "subject only to
such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a
free and democratic society." This is not a loophole so much as a discipline:
it means the state may limit a right, but it must *justify* the limit, with
evidence, under the **Oakes test** set out below. Most Canadian rights
litigation is fought here rather than over whether a right was engaged at all.

**The override is section 33**, the notwithstanding clause. Parliament or a
provincial legislature may declare that a law operates *notwithstanding*
sections 2 and 7 through 15, for renewable five-year terms. It is the
democratic escape hatch from judicial supremacy — and its price is political
rather than legal, since a government using it must say so out loud and face
the voters within five years.

Remedies give it teeth: courts strike down offending laws (s.52),
exclude tainted evidence, and order tailored remedies (s.24). Since
1982 the Charter has remade criminal procedure, legalized abortion
access (*Morgentaler*, 1988 — striking the criminal framework) and
same-sex marriage's path, structured end-of-life law (*Carter*,
2015), and become — polls are consistent — the single most cherished
symbol of Canadian identity, *including in Quebec* (Lesson 23's
paradox). One number for scale: s.15's open "analogous grounds"
design means the equality clause has *grown* since enactment —
the living tree (Lesson 19), planted deliberately.

## Going Deeper

### The Oakes test: how s.1 actually runs

*R. v. Oakes* (1986) turned s.1's sentence into a checklist the
government must satisfy **after** a claimant proves an
infringement:

1. **Pressing and substantial objective** — is the law's goal
   important enough to justify limiting a right?
2. **Proportionality**, three sub-steps:
   a. **Rational connection** — does the measure actually further
      the goal?
   b. **Minimal impairment** — does it limit the right as little
      as reasonably possible? (Most cases die here — "you could
      have achieved this less intrusively.")
   c. **Balance** — do the benefits outweigh the harm to the
      right?

Read the burden allocation: the *citizen* shows a limit; the
*state* justifies it with evidence. That's the Canadian
philosophy in procedural form — rights limitable, limitations
accountable. It's why hate-speech laws (*Keegstra*), advertising
restrictions, and election spending caps coexist with s.2(b)
expression: each survived (or failed) Oakes on its record, not on
absolutism either way.

### Section 33: the override's biography

The bargain's price (Lesson 23) — and for decades, a loaded gun
kept ostentatiously unfired outside Quebec (which used it
routinely 1982–85 in protest, then for signage after *Ford*,
1988 — the "notwithstanding" chapter of Bill 101, Lesson 22).
An informal taboo held: using s.33 was politically radioactive,
which was exactly the design theory — legal availability,
political pricing.

**The taboo is now broken**, and this is the Charter's live
crisis: Quebec pre-emptively shielded its laïcité law (Bill 21,
2019 — religious symbols banned for some public servants) and
language reform (Bill 96, 2022); Ontario invoked it for election-
finance rules (2021) and threatened it for wage caps and a
strike ban (2022 — retreated under union fury); Saskatchewan used
it for school pronoun policy (2023). The emerging pattern —
**pre-emptive** use (invoking before courts even rule) —
neutralizes judicial review entirely, converting "dialogue
between courts and legislatures" into a monologue. The unsettled
questions your newspaper will be arguing about: can courts at
least *declare* rights violated under an override (pending
litigation on Bill 21 points there)? Should s.33 require more
than a majority vote? Notice — Lesson 23's note — that fixing
any of this requires the amending formula, i.e., it won't be
fixed; the contest is over political norms, the only material
Canadian constitutional law is actually made of.

### What the Charter did NOT change

Parliamentary supremacy survives in the seams: s.1 and s.33 as
designed valves; property rights deliberately omitted (unlike
the US 5th Amendment — a CCF-era legacy and provincial demand;
expropriation is statutory politics, not constitutional
litigation); no economic/social rights (housing, health as
rights have repeatedly failed under s.7); and the Charter binds
only *state* action — private discrimination is handled by
ordinary (amendable) human-rights codes, federal and provincial
(Lesson 33's tribunals). Plus s.25's shield: Charter equality
cannot be read to erase Aboriginal and treaty rights
(Lessons 10, 17) — individual and collective rights are held in
deliberate, permanent tension. The design keeps rearming this
course's oldest theme: Canada entrenches balances, not trumps.

{: .note }
> **Reading a Charter headline**
> "Court strikes down X" → a s.52 invalidation, often
> *suspended* for a year to let Parliament redraft (the
> Canadian specialty: declaring the law dead and letting it
> govern during the funeral — dialogue in action; *Carter* and
> *Bedford* ran exactly so). "Government invokes
> notwithstanding clause" → s.33: the law operates regardless;
> the five-year clock starts; the fight moves from courtrooms
> to elections — which is where s.33's authors said it
> belonged, and where its opponents say rights go to die.
> Both sentences are Canadian civics working as designed;
> which design wins is the current decade's question.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* Name the Charter's main categories of
rights and the two clauses that make Canadian rights
"presumptions, not absolutes."

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Categories: fundamental freedoms (s.2 — religion, expression,
assembly, association); democratic rights (ss.3–5); mobility
(s.6); legal rights (ss.7–14 — life/liberty/security, search,
detention, counsel, fair trial); equality (s.15); official-
language and minority-language-education rights (ss.16–23). The
two clauses: s.1 — rights subject to reasonable limits the
government must demonstrably justify (the Oakes test); s.33 —
the notwithstanding clause letting legislatures override s.2
and ss.7–15 for renewable five-year periods. Democratic,
mobility, and language rights cannot be overridden.
</details>

**Q2.** Walk the Oakes test's steps and identify where most laws
fail — and what that reveals about the Charter's deepest effect.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
After a claimant establishes infringement, the government must
show: (1) a pressing and substantial objective; (2)
proportionality — (a) rational connection between means and
goal, (b) minimal impairment (the right limited as little as
reasonably possible), (c) overall balance of benefit over harm.
Most laws die at minimal impairment — the state's goal is
usually fine; its instrument is usually broader than necessary.
Revelation: the Charter's main effect is not striking laws but
disciplining their drafting — governments now design
legislation to survive Oakes (justification memos, tailored
means), so the test governs thousands of laws that never see a
courtroom. Rights review as quality control, not just veto.
</details>

**Q3.** Describe s.33's original political-pricing theory, the
evidence it's failing, and the one clear counter-example.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Theory (1981): the override would be legally easy but
politically ruinous — a government must publicly confess to
overriding rights, face the courts' findings, and survive an
election within the five-year sunset; rarity was the predicted
equilibrium, and for ~35 years (outside Quebec's protest-era and
signage uses) it held. Failing evidence: since 2018–19,
pre-emptive invocations — Quebec's Bill 21 (laïcité) and Bill 96
(language), Saskatchewan's pronoun law, Ontario's election-
finance use — deployed <em>before</em> judicial rulings, at
little measured electoral cost, normalizing the tool and muting
the confession mechanism (no court findings to campaign on).
Counter-example: Ontario 2022 — s.33 attached to a strike-ban
was withdrawn within days under threatened general labour
action: when civil society supplies the political price, the
pricing mechanism still works. Current equilibrium: contested —
which is to say, the Charter's real guarantee is turning out to
be (as its drafters privately knew) the civic culture around
it.
</details>
