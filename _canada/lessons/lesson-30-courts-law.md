# Lesson 30: Courts and the Law

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **common law** — law built from judges' past decisions (**precedent**, PRESS-ih-dent), England's tradition; **civil law** — law organized in one comprehensive written **code**, France's tradition (Quebec's private law).
> - **bijural** (by-JOOR-ul) — running both legal traditions in one country, as Canada does.
> - **tort** (TORT) — a civil wrong (negligence, defamation) you can sue over.
> - **notary** (NOH-tah-ree) — in Quebec, a distinct legal profession handling property, wills, and contracts.
> - **tribunal** (try-BYOO-nul) — a specialized, less formal decision-body (landlord–tenant, human rights) where most everyday disputes actually go.
> - **judicial review** — a court checking whether a government decision or law was legal.
> - **leave to appeal** — a court's *permission* to bring an appeal (the Supreme Court chooses its cases).
> - **stay / stayed** (STAY) — a case halted permanently or temporarily (Jordan stays = charges dismissed for delay).
> - **bail** — release from custody while awaiting trial.
> - **beyond a reasonable doubt** — the criminal standard of proof: near-certainty, not just "probably guilty."
> - **sentencing circle** — an Indigenous-tradition process where community members help shape a sentence.
> - **legal aid** — government-funded lawyers for those who can't pay; **self-represented** — arguing your own case without a lawyer.
> - Names: ***R.* in case names** = Rex/Regina (Latin: the King/Queen — i.e., the Crown prosecuting), **Gladue** (GLAD-yoo), **Nadon** (nah-DON), **Comeau** (KOH-moh), **O'Bonsawin** (oh-BON-sah-win).

## Concept

Canada runs on **three legal traditions in one system**: English
**common law** everywhere except private law in Quebec, which runs on
the **civil law** of the *Code civil du Québec* (the Quebec Act's
bargain, Lesson 10, still operating daily) — and, increasingly
recognized, **Indigenous legal orders** (Lesson 06's Great Law was
law; s.35 and modern agreements are re-making space for the
tradition). One criminal law for the whole country (federal,
Lesson 29); one final court over everything.

Canadian courts form a pyramid, and it is worth climbing it from the bottom,
because that is where nearly everyone actually meets the law.

At the base sit the **provincial courts** — the workhorses of the system.
Roughly 95% of criminal cases start and finish here, along with small claims,
traffic matters, and most family disputes. Their judges are provincially
appointed. Beside them, and just as important in practice, are the
**administrative tribunals**: landlord-tenant boards, labour boards, human
rights commissions, the refugee board. If you ever deal with "the law" in
Canada, this is overwhelmingly the level you will deal with.

Above them are the **superior courts**, one per province, handling serious
crime, large civil cases, and family law. Note a Confederation quirk here: the
courts are provincial, but their judges are appointed *federally*. Running
alongside is the **Federal Court**, which handles immigration, intellectual
property, and judicial review of federal agencies, together with the **Tax
Court**.

Above those sit the **provincial courts of appeal** and the **Federal Court of
Appeal**. And at the apex is the **Supreme Court of Canada**: nine judges, of
whom three must by law come from Quebec's civil-law bar — the direct
descendant of the Quebec Act's bargain in Lesson 10. For most cases you cannot
simply appeal to it; you must first be granted *leave*, and the Court takes
only a small fraction of what is asked of it.

The cultural headline, and this lesson's through-line: **Canadian
judicial appointments are boring, and that is a national
achievement.** No elections, no confirmation circus, no campaign
donations to judges — and consequently, courts that most Canadians
(and both political camps, most of the time) simply accept as
legitimate. How that boringness is engineered, and what it buys, is
worth understanding precisely because so few countries manage it.

## Going Deeper

### The traditions

- **Common law** (all provinces/territories except Quebec's private
  law): judge-made precedent accumulating case by case — contracts,
  torts, property evolve through decisions; statutes overlay.
- **Civil law** (Quebec private law): a comprehensive code —
  principles first, cases as application; the Code civil (1994,
  succeeding the 1866 code) governs family, property, contracts,
  civil liability. Practical consequences: notaries as a distinct
  profession, different matrimonial-property defaults, and a
  distinct legal culture — which is why **three of nine SCC seats
  are statutorily Quebec's** (the Court must be able to hear civil
  appeals competently — and the 2014 *Nadon* reference held even
  this composition rule constitutionally entrenched).
- **Indigenous legal orders**: from *Delgamuukw*'s oral-history
  evidence (Lesson 06) through s.35 jurisprudence (Lesson 24's
  arc) to modern self-government agreements and Indigenous courts
  (sentencing circles; *Gladue* principles — judges must consider
  Indigenous offenders' circumstances and alternatives to prison,
  a direct response to Lesson 24's incarceration numbers). Status
  honestly: recognition growing, integration genuinely unsettled —
  the third tradition is being rebuilt from suppression
  (Lesson 17 banned its practitioners' institutions for a century).

### The criminal side (one Code, provincial delivery)

Federal Criminal Code; provincial administration (police,
prosecutions, courts — Lesson 29). The essentials a citizen should
know: **presumption of innocence** (Charter s.11(d)); Crown must
prove **beyond a reasonable doubt**; no capital punishment
(abolished 1976; last hanging 1962); jury trials for serious
charges (12 jurors, unanimity for conviction — Lesson 33 walks
your jury duty); bail as the rule, detention the exception
(s.11(e), perpetually contested in practice); and sentencing
philosophy that weighs rehabilitation alongside denunciation —
Canadian incarceration runs ~⅙ the US rate (with Lesson 24's
caveat: the composition of who *is* incarcerated is the system's
standing indictment).

### How judges are appointed (the boring machine)

Provincial-court judges: provincial processes with screening
committees. Superior courts: **federal** appointment (1867's
centralizing quirk) through advisory committees vetting
applications — lawyers apply, literally, with forms. The Supreme
Court since 2016: open applications, an independent advisory
board, published shortlist criteria (functional bilingualism now
expected), a public but non-adversarial parliamentary Q&A with
the nominee — no vote, no veto, no attack ads. Judges hold
office to 75 on good behaviour; removal requires joint
parliamentary address (never used federally — the Canadian
Judicial Council process has always resolved matters short of
it). Salaries set by independent commission (buying judges is
structurally hard); the bench is drawn overwhelmingly from
senior practitioners, not politics — the last SCC judge with a
partisan elected past retired decades ago.

What the design buys: no judicial "wings" tracked by
appointing-party, no court-packing discourse, decisions
criticized on merits rather than provenance, and — the deep
metric — losing parties comply without drama, including
governments losing constitutional cases they hated (patriation
1981, secession 1998, carbon 2021: each time, the loser obeyed
a court it had every political incentive to delegitimize). The
honest caveats: opacity has its own costs (diversity on the
bench lagged for decades — the first Indigenous SCC justice,
Michelle O'Bonsawin, arrived 2022); "non-political" appointment
still tilts toward establishment lawyers; and the Charter era
(Lesson 31) has made judges *more* powerful while their
selection stays quiet — a legitimacy trade Canada mostly
declines to examine, because examining it is how you get the
American disease. Boring is a strategy, and strategies have
prices.

### Access to justice — the system's real crisis

The pyramid's dirty secret: most Canadians can't afford its
middle floors. Superior-court civil litigation runs tens of
thousands of dollars; legal aid covers only the poorest and
mostly criminal/family emergencies; the response is the growth
of **small claims courts** (up to $35,000 in Ontario — designed
for self-representation), **administrative tribunals**
(landlord–tenant, employment standards, human-rights — where
ordinary life's disputes actually go), self-represented
litigants clogging family courts, and delay so severe the SCC
imposed constitutional trial deadlines (*Jordan*, 2016 — cases
die if the system dawdles). When Lesson 33 tours "the justice
system in practice," this — not the marble courtroom — is the
practice.

{: .note }
> **Reading a Canadian case name**
> *R. v. Comeau*: R is Rex/Regina — the Crown (Lesson 25)
> prosecutes all crimes; criminal cases are "the King versus."
> Civil: *Smith v. Jones*. References — *Reference re Secession
> of Quebec* — are the Canadian specialty: governments may ask
> courts advisory constitutional questions directly (no
> case-or-controversy limit as in the US), which is why so many
> of this course's landmarks (patriation, secession, Senate,
> carbon) arrived as References: Canada asks first, fights
> after — occasionally even listens.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* Describe Canada's court pyramid from
bottom to top, and state the special composition rule of the
Supreme Court.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Provincial courts (the volume: most criminal, traffic, small
claims, much family) and administrative tribunals → superior
courts of each province (serious crime, major civil, divorce) and
the parallel Federal Court/Tax Court (federal matters) → provincial
courts of appeal and the Federal Court of Appeal → the Supreme
Court of Canada: nine judges, appeals mostly by leave, final on
everything. Composition rule: three of the nine must come from
Quebec (civil-law qualified) — a statutory requirement the Court
itself held constitutionally entrenched (Nadon reference, 2014),
protecting the bijural bargain of 1774/1867.
</details>

**Q2.** Name four features of Canadian criminal justice a newcomer
should know, and the doctrine addressing Indigenous
over-incarceration.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Any four: presumption of innocence with proof beyond a reasonable
doubt; one federal Criminal Code nationwide (no provincial crime
patchwork); no death penalty (abolished 1976); jury of 12 with
unanimous verdict for serious charges; bail as the constitutional
default; constitutional trial-delay limits (Jordan, 2016);
incarceration rates a fraction of the US's. The doctrine: Gladue
(1999, Criminal Code s.718.2(e)) — sentencing judges must consider
the systemic background of Indigenous offenders and all
reasonable alternatives to imprisonment, with "Gladue reports"
informing courts; a direct judicial response to the incarceration
statistics of Lesson 24 (necessary, and — by those same
statistics' persistence — insufficient).
</details>

**Q3.** What is a reference case, why is it distinctively useful,
and name three landmark References this course has already met.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
A government (federal → SCC directly; provincial → its court of
appeal, then SCC) asks the courts an advisory constitutional
question without waiting for concrete litigation. Usefulness:
constitutional crises get answered before they detonate —
rules of the game clarified in advance, at the cost of courts
opining in the abstract. Landmarks met so far: the Patriation
Reference (1981 — legal but conventionally improper, forcing the
final conference, Lesson 23); the Secession Reference (1998 — no
unilateral secession, but clear-majority-on-clear-question
obliges negotiation, Lesson 22); the carbon-pricing reference
(2021 — federal backstop upheld under POGG, Lesson 29); plus the
Persons Case itself (Lesson 19) and the Senate and Nadon
references (Lesson 26, this lesson). Note the pattern: Canada's
biggest constitutional moments are questions asked politely in
advance — the national style, in litigation form.
</details>
