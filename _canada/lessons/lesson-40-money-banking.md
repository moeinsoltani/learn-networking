# Lesson 40: Money and Banking

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **central bank** — the public institution that manages a country's money and interest rates; **monetary policy** — that management.
> - **overnight (policy) rate** — the interest rate the central bank sets; every other rate in the economy prices off it; **prime rate** — the banks' base lending rate, which follows it.
> - **inflation targeting** — publicly promising to keep inflation at a number (2%) and steering rates to hit it.
> - **lender of last resort** — the central bank's role of lending to banks in a panic so the system doesn't collapse.
> - **mortgage term** vs **amortization** (am-or-tih-ZAY-shun) — the term is how long your *rate* is locked (in Canada, usually ≤5 years); amortization is the total years to pay the loan off (~25). At each term's end you *renew* at current rates — this is why Canadian rate hikes hit fast.
> - **stress test** — the rule that you must qualify at a higher rate than you'll actually pay, to prove you can survive hikes.
> - **recourse mortgage** — the bank can pursue your other assets if you walk away (unlike much of the US).
> - **petro-currency** — a currency that rises and falls with oil prices; **loonie / toonie** — the $1 and $2 coins (and nicknames for the dollar itself).
> - **CMHC** — the government mortgage insurer; **OSFI** (OSS-fee) — the bank regulator.
> - Names: **Coyne** (KOYN), **Viola Desmond** (vy-OH-lah DEZ-mund — the civil-rights pioneer on the $10 bill).

## Concept

Three institutions to understand, one national conversation to
decode:

Three pieces make up the Canadian money system, and each one has a distinctly
Canadian character.

**The Bank of Canada**, founded in 1935 out of the Depression (Lesson 19), has
one mandate that really matters: **keep inflation at 2%**, within a 1–3% band,
under an agreement renewed with the government every five years since 1991 —
which made Canada one of the world's first inflation-targeting economies. Its
main tool is the **overnight rate**, announced on eight fixed dates a year;
those announcements are the news events that move every mortgage in the
country. The Bank is independent in its operations but accountable for its
mandate — the governor is emphatically not a politician, a boundary settled by
the Coyne Affair of 1961. It also prints the money, runs the payment rails,
and acts as lender of last resort.

**The Big Five banks** — RBC, TD, Scotiabank, BMO, and CIBC, with National
Bank often making it six — form an **oligopoly by design**, not by accident.
Canadian banks have been allowed to branch nationally since 1867, unlike the
fragmented American unit-banking tradition; entry barriers are high;
"widely held" ownership rules prevent takeovers; and mergers have been
blocked outright, most famously in 1998 when the finance minister simply said
no. The trade-off is the one you will see everywhere in Canadian life:
**stability bought with concentration** — remarkably few bank failures, and
remarkably high fees.

**The loonie** has floated since 1970, and it behaves like a **petro-currency**:
it tracks the oil price, which is Lesson 05's staples thesis showing up in the
foreign-exchange market. It is also a small-open-economy currency, which means
the Bank of Canada's rate decisions are always made in the shadow of the US
Federal Reserve's — Lesson 38's elephant, monetary edition.

The national conversation: **housing** — the largest industry
(Lesson 37), financed by these banks, insured
substantially by the state (CMHC), rate-priced by the Bank, and
tax-advantaged into a pension substitute (Lesson 35). Money,
banks, and housing are one braided system in Canada; this lesson
braids them.

## Going Deeper

### The Bank of Canada: how the 2% machine works

Born of the Depression (Lesson 19 — private-money orthodoxy's
failure), nationalized 1938. The modern regime since 1991:
**inflation targeting** — 2% midpoint, jointly renewed with the
elected government every five years (independence *within* a
democratic mandate: the Canadian valve design of Lesson 31,
applied to money). Mechanics for the citizen: the **overnight
rate** ripples outward — prime rate moves within hours →
variable mortgages and lines of credit reprice immediately →
fixed mortgages price off bond yields anticipating the path →
the dollar moves with rate differentials vs the Fed. Eight
announcement dates a year; the *Monetary Policy Report*
quarterly; the governor's press conferences parsed like scripture.
Track record, honestly: ~30 years of inflation near 2% (a genuine
institutional success story), then the 2021–23 surge to 8% —
pandemic supply + stimulus — answered by the fastest hiking cycle
in decades (0.25% → 5%), which broke inflation *and* stress-tested
every mortgage in the country (below). The episode's civic lesson:
the 2% promise is credible because it was painful to keep — and
the Bank chose the pain.

### Why Canadian banking is boring (a 150-year design)

The 1867 constitution made banking federal (Lesson 29 — one
regulator, no state-bank patchwork); charters favoured few, large,
**nationally branched** banks — so Canadian banks diversify
across regions by default (an Alberta bust is an Ontario branch's
offset — Lesson 37's regional seesaw as a *stabilizer*), where
US unit banking bred thousands of fragile local banks (9,000+ US
failures in the Depression; Canada: **zero**). Add conservative
regulation (OSFI), "widely held" ownership rules (no single owner
controls a big bank), and the 1998 merger refusals, and you get
the famous 2008 result: **no Canadian bank failed or was bailed
out** (liquidity support, yes; collapse, no) — the IMF and the
World Economic Forum spent a decade citing Canada as the world's
soundest system. The bill for the boringness, per Lesson 37:
oligopoly pricing (among the rich world's higher account fees and
mutual-fund costs), slow fintech adoption (open banking arriving
years behind peers), and a mortgage book concentrated on one
asset class — which brings us to:

### Housing: the system's chosen exposure

The pieces, assembled: **CMHC** (1946 — the Crown insurer:
mortgages under 20% down *must* carry government-backed
insurance, socializing tail risk to enable lending); the
**stress test** (since 2016–18: qualify at ~2% above your
contract rate — the regulator pre-breaking the fever); the
**shorter-term structure** — Canadian "fixed" mortgages fix for
*five years or less* on ~25-year amortizations (nothing like the
US 30-year fixed), so **rate hikes reach households fast**: the
2022–23 cycle repriced a third of mortgages within two years
(payment shocks of 30–60%), the precise transmission channel
that made Canadian housing the Bank's brake pad; **household
debt ~180% of disposable income** (top of the G7 — the
vulnerability every IMF report underlines); and the political
economy from Lesson 35/37: two-thirds of households are owners
whose net worth *is* the house, so policy defends prices while
lamenting affordability — the national straddle. Honest system
verdict: the banks are genuinely robust to a housing correction
(capital, insurance, recourse mortgages — Canadian borrowers
can't mail in the keys like Americans); the *households* and the
*economy* (construction + real-estate GDP share) are the exposed
parties. The boring banking system's un-boring twin is the
balance sheet of the Canadian family.

### The loonie

Floating since 1970 (early and proudly — Canada pioneered
floating among majors); named for the bird on the $1 coin
(1987; the $2 "toonie" followed 1996 — Canadian nomenclature
peaked early). A **commodity currency**: oil and the terms of
trade move it (Lesson 05's lab ran this), typically 70–90 US
cents in the modern era; the discount is Ontario
manufacturing's subsidy and the snowbird's lament. Polymer
notes since 2011 (they survive washing machines and melt on
radiators); the $20 carries the monarch (Lesson 25); Viola
Desmond — the Black Nova Scotian businesswoman whose 1946
cinema arrest prefigured the civil-rights era — carries the
$10: Lesson 45's symbols file, in your wallet.

{: .note }
> **The Coyne Affair (1961) — why the governor can say no**
> Diefenbaker's government wanted easier money; Governor James
> Coyne disagreed publicly; the government legislated to fire
> him; the Senate (Lesson 26, earning its keep) refused; Coyne,
> vindicated, resigned anyway. The settlement that emerged —
> the government may issue a public *directive* overriding the
> Bank (never used: issuing one means owning the consequences),
> otherwise the Bank runs policy — is Lesson 25's reserve-power
> pattern exactly: a nuclear option whose non-use is the
> system, deterrent ambiguity keeping both sides honest. When
> politicians attack the Bank (it recurs), the Coyne
> settlement is the constitutional floor under the governor's
> feet.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* What is the Bank of Canada, what
is its main job, and why do its eight annual announcements
dominate the news?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Canada's central bank (est. 1935): it issues the currency,
runs payment systems, acts as lender of last resort, and —
the main job — keeps inflation at the 2% target (1–3% band)
set jointly with the government since 1991, using the
overnight policy rate announced on eight fixed dates yearly.
The announcements dominate news because Canada's mortgage
structure (five-year-or-shorter terms, many variable) means
rate changes reach household budgets within months — a rate
decision is, for millions, a direct change to the month's
largest bill; plus the dollar, business lending, and housing
prices all reprice off the same number.
</details>

**Q2.** Why did no Canadian bank fail in 1929–33 or 2008, and
what is the acknowledged price of that record?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Design: federal jurisdiction produced few, large, nationally
branched banks — regionally diversified by default (a
prairie bust offset by central-Canadian branches), unlike
the US's thousands of fragile local unit banks; add
conservative regulation (OSFI), widely-held ownership rules,
blocked mergers (1998), and mandatory insurance of high-ratio
mortgages (CMHC). Results: zero Depression failures (vs
9,000+ American) and 2008 passed without a Canadian bank
failure or equity bailout — the "world's soundest banks"
decade of citations. The price: an oligopoly's rents — high
fees and spreads, slow innovation and fintech entry, limited
consumer choice — plus a mortgage-concentrated asset book
whose risk sits, via household debt (~180% of income), on
family balance sheets rather than bank ones. Stability was
purchased with competition; Canadians pay the invoice
monthly.
</details>

**Q3.** Why is the Canadian dollar called a petro-currency, and
who wins/loses when it falls?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Because it tracks Canada's terms of trade, dominated by oil:
higher oil → export receipts and investment flows strengthen
the loonie; oil busts sink it (Lesson 05's staples logic in
foreign-exchange form). A falling loonie: winners — Ontario/
Quebec manufacturers and exporters (US customers pay in
suddenly-cheaper Canadian terms; L.38's 75% makes this
first-order), tourism operators, film production (L.39's
Hollywood North exists partly on the discount); losers —
consumers (imports, groceries — winter produce is priced in
USD), snowbirds and travellers, and businesses importing
equipment (a productivity-investment tax, feeding L.37's
gap). The permanent pattern: the exchange rate is the
mechanism by which Alberta's boom taxes Ontario's factories
and vice versa — one currency, two economies, forever
seesawing.
</details>
