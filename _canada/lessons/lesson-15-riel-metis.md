# Lesson 15: Riel and the Métis Resistances

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **provisional government** — a temporary government formed to run things until a permanent, recognized one exists.
> - **high treason** (TREE-zun) — the crime of making war against your own country's Crown; the most serious offence in the law.
> - **court-martial** (court-MAR-shul) — a military court trial; **the gallows** (GAL-ohz) — the structure used for hanging.
> - **scrip** (SKRIP) — a paper certificate the government gave Métis people, exchangeable for land or money; most were bought up cheaply by speculators.
> - **speculator** — someone who buys assets cheaply hoping to resell at profit.
> - **Orangeman** — a member of the Orange Order, a militantly Protestant, anti-Catholic society powerful in old Ontario.
> - **amnesty** (AM-nes-tee) — an official pardon for political offences; **exile** — forced absence from your country.
> - **asylum** (uh-SY-lum) — here: a psychiatric hospital (old usage).
> - **martyr** (MAR-ter) — someone killed for a cause, honoured by its supporters.
> - **exonerated** (eg-ZON-er-ay-ted) — officially cleared of guilt; **posthumously** (POSS-chuh-muss-lee) — after death.
> - Names: **Riel** (ree-EL), **Métis** (may-TEE), **Batoche** (bah-TOSH), **Gabriel Dumont** (doo-MON), **Poundmaker** (Cree: Pîtikwahanapiwiyin), **Mercier** (mair-see-AY), **Assiniboine** (uh-SIN-ih-boyn).

## Concept

No figure in Canadian history carries more contradictory labels than
**Louis Riel**: traitor, hanged for high treason in 1885 — and Father of
Confederation (Manitoba's founder), folk hero, martyr, and, since 1992 by
parliamentary resolution, founder of Manitoba honoured across the country
with statues and a provincial holiday (**Louis Riel Day**). Same man, same
acts. This lesson is about the two armed Métis resistances he led — and
about why his reputation flipped, which teaches you as much about Canada
as the events themselves.

There were two resistances, sixteen years apart, and they ended in opposite
ways — which is the first thing to get straight.

**Red River, 1869–70**, began when Canada bought Rupert's Land without
consulting a single person living on it (Lesson 14). The Métis under Riel
stopped the surveyors, turned back the incoming governor, and formed a
**provisional government** — a move in a genuine legal grey zone, and
arguably legitimate, since no Canadian authority yet existed there to
displace. It ended in *negotiation*: the Manitoba Act of 1870 created a
province with land promises and bilingual rights. But the execution of a
prisoner named Thomas Scott poisoned Ontario against Riel, and he was driven
into exile despite being elected to Parliament three times.

**The North-West, 1885**, began with the same grievance and worse conditions.
The Métis had moved west to the Saskatchewan valley, and there they and their
Cree and Assiniboine neighbours faced starvation and years of ignored
petitions. Riel returned from exile and declared a provisional government at
Batoche — but this time it was war: Duck Lake, Fish Creek, and finally Batoche
in May 1885. It ended in *defeat*, a trial, and the gallows at Regina on
November 16, 1885. Quebec mourned a martyr; Ontario cheered justice; and the
crack that opened between them ran straight through Confederation's founding
partnership.

The through-line: **both resistances were, at bottom, about consent** —
the same question the Royal Proclamation (Lesson 10) had answered on
paper a century earlier: can the people living on the land be
transferred, surveyed, and settled without being asked?

## Going Deeper

### Red River, 1869–70: resistance that worked

The sale of Rupert's Land (Lesson 14) was set to transfer the Red River
Settlement — ~12,000 people, majority Métis (French- and
English-speaking), organized around Lesson 09's hunt-and-cart economy —
to Canadian rule with no consultation, while Canadian surveyors were
already re-gridding their riverfront lots (long, thin, seigneurial-style
— Lesson 08's pattern transplanted west) into Ontario-style squares.
The Métis, led by the 25-year-old, convent-and-college-educated,
bilingual **Louis Riel**, stopped the surveys, turned back the incoming
governor, seized Fort Garry (Winnipeg) without bloodshed, and formed a
provisional government with a bill of rights, inviting the settlement's
anglophone parishes in.

It largely *worked*: Ottawa negotiated, and the **Manitoba Act (1870)**
created the province — tiny ("the postage-stamp province"), but with
French/English official bilingualism, denominational schools, and
**s.31: 1.4 million acres reserved for Métis children**. Then the poison
pill: Riel's government court-martialed and executed **Thomas Scott**, a
violent Ontario Orangeman prisoner — a legally defensible, politically
catastrophic act. Ontario put a bounty on Riel; the military expedition
sent west harassed the Métis; the s.31 lands were delivered through a
scrip system so slow and fraud-riddled that most Métis lands passed to
speculators (in 2013 the Supreme Court held the Crown had failed its
honour on exactly this promise). Manitoba's Métis majority became a
dispossessed minority within a decade; many moved west to the
Saskatchewan valley — where the story repeats, harder. Riel, though
elected to Parliament three times, could never take his seat; amnestied
on condition of five years' exile, he ended up a schoolteacher in
Montana — increasingly gripped by a religious mission (he had spent
1876–78 in Quebec asylums; his sanity is the trial's central knot).

### The North-West, 1885: resistance that was crushed

By 1884 the South Saskatchewan Métis faced the same unanswered land
petitions, and the Plains Cree and Assiniboine faced worse: the
**buffalo were gone** (functionally extinct by 1879), the promised
treaty rations (Lesson 17) were being used as leverage — food withheld
to force compliance — and people were starving. A Métis delegation rode
700 miles to Montana to fetch Riel.

What followed, March–May 1885: a declared provisional government at
**Batoche**; first blood at **Duck Lake** (Métis victory under the
brilliant hunt-captain **Gabriel Dumont**); separate Cree actions
(Frog Lake; chiefs **Poundmaker** and **Big Bear** — both of whom had
spent years pursuing peaceful diplomacy and largely *restrained* their
warriors — swept up regardless); Ottawa's 5,000 troops arriving in nine
days by the new CPR (Lesson 14's grateful Parliament); and the
three-day **Battle of Batoche**, ending with the Métis out of ammunition,
firing nails and stones. Dumont escaped to the U.S. (and, for a time,
Buffalo Bill's Wild West show — history is strange); Riel surrendered.

### The trial and the fracture

Riel was tried in Regina for **high treason** — under the medieval
Statute of Treasons, carrying a mandatory death penalty — before a
six-man anglophone Protestant jury. His lawyers' only viable defence was
insanity; **Riel refused it**, arguing lucidly that his cause was sane
and just (his final speech: "I know that through the grace of God I am
the founder of Manitoba"). Guilty — with the jury's recommendation of
mercy. Macdonald, pressed from Ontario to hang him and Quebec to spare
him: *"He shall hang though every dog in Quebec bark in his favour."*
November 16, 1885, Regina. Poundmaker and Big Bear got three years each
(both died broken shortly after release); eight Cree and Assiniboine men
were hanged at Battleford in Canada's largest mass execution, with
students from the new Battleford residential school marched out to
watch — a sentence you should read twice.

Riel's own words at that trial are worth reading, because they explain why he
chose to die. Addressing the jury on July 31, 1885:

> "When I came into the North-West… I found the Indians suffering. I found the
> half-breeds eating the rotten pork of the Hudson's Bay Company and getting
> sick and weak every day… I directed my attention to help the Indians, to help
> the half-breeds and directed my attention to plead their cause with the
> Government."

And later, flatly:

> "I know that through the grace of God I am the founder of Manitoba."

Look at what that speech is doing. **Legally**, it is suicide by lucidity. His
counsel's insanity defence required him to appear deranged, and he
methodically demonstrates the opposite — organized narrative, dates, causes,
consequences — because being acquitted as a madman would have converted the
Métis cause itself into a symptom. He chose the gallows over the asylum in
order to keep the grievance *sane*.

**Politically**, it redirects the trial. The question "did Riel levy war
against the Crown" was undeniable and therefore useless to him; so he replaces
it with "what drove starving communities to war after years of lawful
petition," putting the government's administration of the North-West in the
dock instead.

**Historically**, he is drafting his own verdict for a later court. In 1885,
"founder of Manitoba" was an outrageous thing for a convicted traitor to call
himself. It is now the literal caption on his statue at the Manitoba
Legislature. The speech is one of the rare documents in which you can watch a
man lose his case and win his history.

**The fracture:** Quebec saw a French Catholic hanged for leading his
people; 50,000 rallied on the Champ de Mars; **Honoré Mercier** rode the
fury to Quebec's premiership, founding the tradition of Quebec
nationalist provincial politics — the direct ancestor of everything in
Lesson 22. Ontario celebrated. The Macdonald–Cartier partnership's
successors would spend the next century managing the crack that opened
November 16, 1885.

{: .note }
> **Why Riel's reputation flipped**
> Nothing about 1869–85 changed; Canada did. As the country stopped
> reading itself as a British project and started reckoning with
> Indigenous dispossession (Phase 4's arc), Riel migrated from margin to
> founder: the man who forced negotiated provincehood (the only province
> created by Indigenous-led action), whose "treason" was defending the
> Proclamation-era principle of consent. His statue now stands at the
> Manitoba Legislature; Manitoba's February holiday bears his name; his
> hanging is taught — as here — as the miscarriage. When a country
> re-labels its traitors founders, study the country, not just the man.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* Who was Louis Riel, and why is he called
the founder of Manitoba?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
The Métis leader who headed the Red River Resistance of 1869–70 —
blocking Canada's unconsulted takeover of Rupert's Land and forming a
provisional government — whose negotiations with Ottawa produced the
Manitoba Act of 1870, creating the province with bilingual rights and
Métis land promises. He later led the 1885 North-West Resistance in
Saskatchewan, was convicted of high treason, and was hanged in Regina on
November 16, 1885. Once officially a traitor, he is now formally
recognized as Manitoba's founder; the province's February civic holiday
is Louis Riel Day.
</details>

**Q2.** What conditions produced the 1885 North-West Resistance — for
the Métis and for the Cree — and what role did the CPR play in its
defeat?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Métis: the Red River pattern repeating — land petitions from the South
Saskatchewan settlements ignored for years, surveys threatening river
lots, and the Manitoba land promises (s.31 scrip) already lost to delay
and speculators. Cree/Assiniboine: catastrophe — the buffalo's
extermination ended the plains economy, and treaty-promised rations were
administered punitively (withheld to force bands onto reserves),
producing genuine starvation; chiefs like Poundmaker and Big Bear had
pursued diplomacy for years before events overran them. The CPR moved
~5,000 troops west in nine days (vs months overland in 1870), crushing
the resistance within weeks at Batoche — and the demonstration saved the
nearly bankrupt railway, as Parliament voted its rescue loans in
gratitude. Postscript: Poundmaker was posthumously exonerated by Canada
in 2019.
</details>

**Q3.** How did Riel's execution reshape *Quebec* politics, and why is
that among its most important consequences?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Quebec read the hanging as proof that French Catholics would be
sacrificed to Ontario opinion whenever the two collided — Macdonald's
"every dog in Quebec" line confirming the weighting. The immediate
result: mass rallies, the collapse of Conservative Quebec, and Honoré
Mercier's election as premier on an explicitly nationalist platform —
the founding of provincial-nationalist politics as Quebec's default
posture toward Ottawa. That posture — Quebec as guardian-state of a
minority people within Confederation — runs in a straight line through
the conscription crises (Lessons 18, 20), Duplessis, the Quiet
Revolution, and the referendums (Lesson 22). The 1885 gallows in Regina
is, by wide agreement, where the Confederation bargain of 1867 first
cracked — making Riel's execution as consequential for French–English
relations as for Indigenous ones: one rope, two national wounds.
</details>
