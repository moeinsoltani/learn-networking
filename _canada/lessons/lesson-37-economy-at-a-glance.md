# Lesson 37: The Economy at a Glance

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **GDP** — Gross Domestic Product: the total value of everything a country produces in a year; **per capita** (per KAP-ih-tah) — per person.
> - **G7** — the group of seven big rich democracies (Canada, US, UK, France, Germany, Italy, Japan).
> - **productivity** — output produced per hour of work; the source of long-run wages.
> - **services economy** — one where most people work in offices, shops, health, finance — not farms or factories.
> - **oligopoly** (ol-ih-GOP-uh-lee) — a market controlled by a few big firms (Lesson 04).
> - **branch plant** — a local factory or subsidiary owned by a foreign parent company, which keeps the head office, research, and profits at home.
> - **commercialization** — turning research into products and companies that make money.
> - **multiplier** — the extra economic activity one job or industry generates around it.
> - **capital** (in economics) — the machines, software, and buildings workers use; "investing in capital" = equipping workers better.
> - **export account** — what a country sells abroad (can look very different from what its people work at).
> - **brain drain** — losing your most skilled people to a richer country.

## Concept

Canada is a **G7 economy of ~41 million people** — roughly the
world's ninth-or-tenth largest, top-tier rich by any global
standard — whose self-image and reality have drifted apart in
instructive ways:

Canada's self-image is lumberjacks, oil rigs, and wheat — Lesson 05's "hewers
of wood." The GDP figures say something quite different: **services are more
than 70% of the economy** (finance, real estate, health, education, tech,
retail, government), with resources contributing roughly 10% directly and
manufacturing about the same.

And yet the old image is not wrong where it counts. Look at **exports** rather
than GDP and the picture flips: energy is by far the number-one export, and
adding minerals, lumber, and farm goods leaves resources earning an enormous
share of the foreign money Canada takes in. The resolution is this: **Canada
works in services and pays its way abroad with staples.** Harold Innis
(Lesson 05) loses on the GDP account and wins on the trade account.

The second correction is regional. There is no such thing as "the" Canadian
economy:

| Region | What it runs on |
|---|---|
| **Alberta, Saskatchewan, Newfoundland** | Petro-economies — booms and busts (Lesson 05) |
| **Ontario** | Manufacturing, finance, and tech |
| **Quebec** | Hydroelectricity, aerospace, AI, dairy |
| **British Columbia** | Trade gateway, forestry, real estate |
| **Manitoba** | Diversified and comparatively stable |
| **The Maritimes** | Seafood, shipbuilding — and the export of people, who send money home |
| **The North** | Mining and government |

And one number to carry into every economic conversation: the
**productivity gap** — Canadian GDP per hour runs ~70–75% of the
US level, a gap that widened in the 2010s–20s. It is the national
economic anxiety (the modern version of Lesson 05's "hewers"
worry), and its suspects — small protected markets, oligopoly
comfort, capital fleeing to housing, branch-plant R&D — recur
through this whole phase.

## Going Deeper

### The real proportions

Services dominate output and employment (like every rich country):
finance/insurance/real estate the largest bloc (and real estate's
share — the largest single industry by GDP in the 2020s — is
itself a symptom, Lesson 40); public-adjacent sectors (health,
education, government) enormous per Lesson 34's architecture;
tech significant and growing (Lesson 39). Goods production —
manufacturing ~10% of GDP (halved since the 1970s, the standard
rich-country arc plus China shock), construction outsized (housing
again), resources ~8–10% directly *but* with multipliers: the
oil-sands job in Fort McMurray carries the Newfoundland
fly-in worker (Lesson 05), the Toronto bank's energy desk, the
Edmonton machine shop. The export account is where staples still
rule: energy roughly a quarter of goods exports (the #1 item),
vehicles/parts second (one integrated industry with the US,
Lesson 38), then metals, forest, farm. Summary sentence: **Canada
is a services economy wearing a resource exporter's jersey — and
both facts are real: the services feed Canadians; the staples pay
the world.**

### The branch-plant inheritance

The National Policy (Lesson 14) built manufacturing behind
tariffs — much of it *American subsidiaries* jumping the wall
(GM Canada, IBM Canada...): the **branch-plant economy**. Legacy
patterns that outlived the tariffs: head offices, R&D, and
decision rights concentrated abroad; Canadian operations as
sales-and-assembly; a thin layer of globally scaled Canadian-
headquartered firms (the banks, the railways, Brookfield, Shopify
— the exceptions that prove it). The modern echo: Canada produces
world-class research (AI's godfathers worked in Toronto,
Montreal, Edmonton — Lesson 39) whose commercialization
disproportionately happens, and accrues, elsewhere. "Invented
here, scaled there" is the branch plant's ghost.

### The oligopoly pattern

Lesson 04 predicted it from geography: few, separated markets →
concentrated industries. The roster Canadians grumble about
daily: **five banks** (Lesson 40 — stability's flip side),
**three telecoms** (world-podium mobile prices), **two
airlines**, **two railways**, **a handful of grocers**
(bread-price-fixing scandal included). The honest double
ledger: concentration bought stability (2008, Lesson 40) and
national-scale service across brutal geography — and it charges
for the favour, in prices and in the competitive slack that
feeds the productivity gap. The Competition Bureau's
strengthening (2022–24 reforms) is the current counter-move;
the structure is older than the referee.

### The productivity file (the honest version)

GDP per capita: rich but slipping relative to the US frontier;
GDP per hour: ~three-quarters of US. Standard suspect list:
(1) **capital shallowing** — Canadian firms invest less per
worker (machinery, software, IP), with national savings
detouring into housing (Lesson 35's exemption, Lesson 40's
market); (2) **scale and protection** — internal trade barriers
(Lesson 29), supply management, telecom/banking/air
protectionism breed comfortable mid-size firms that never face
the sharpening gale; (3) **branch-plant R&D** (above);
(4) **resource-mix arithmetic** — some of the 2010s gap is
oil-price and composition effects, not sloth; (5) the newest:
**population arithmetic** — the 2020s immigration surge grew
denominators (people, hours) faster than numerators (capital,
housing, output per hour), mechanically depressing per-capita
figures (Lesson 24's recalibration context). Why care: 
productivity is the wage fund — Lesson 41's US salary gap and
the brain-drain conversation are this file's human face.

{: .note }
> **Why a rich country worries constantly**
> Visitors find Canadian productivity-anxiety puzzling — the
> country is safe, rich, and ranked near the top of everything.
> The worry has structure: (1) the comparator is the
> planet's most dynamic economy, 200 metres away, speaking the
> same language and paying 30–50% more for the same engineer
> (Lesson 41) — Canada is the only rich country whose talent
> can defect by driving south; (2) the welfare state
> (Lesson 34) is funded by tomorrow's productivity — the gap is
> a pension problem wearing an economics costume; (3) history:
> a staples economy (L.05) knows its prosperity rides cycles it
> doesn't control, so the "what do we do when the staple
> fades?" question is four centuries old (cod → fur → wheat →
> oil → ?). The anxiety is, in the Canadian way, both
> overwrought and correct.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* Is Canada's economy primarily
resources, manufacturing, or services — and why does the answer
depend on which account you read?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
By output and employment: services (~70%+ — finance, real
estate, health, education, retail, tech), like all rich
economies. By exports: resources still lead — energy is the #1
export, with minerals, forest, and farm products earning a
disproportionate share of foreign exchange (the staples
tradition, Lesson 05, alive on the trade account).
Manufacturing (~10% of GDP, concentrated in Ontario/Quebec)
dominates neither but anchors the US-integrated auto and
aerospace sectors. So: Canadians mostly work in services;
Canada mostly pays the world with staples; and politics often
behaves as if the country were the export account.
</details>

**Q2.** Name the standard suspects in Canada's productivity gap
and the stake that makes it more than an economists' quarrel.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Suspects: low business investment per worker (capital
shallowing), with savings diverted to housing; weak competitive
pressure in protected oligopolies (banking, telecom, airlines,
dairy) and internal trade barriers limiting scale; branch-plant
structure sending R&D and commercialization abroad;
composition/price effects from the resource mix; and 2020s
population growth outpacing capital and housing. Stakes: output
per hour is the wage fund and the tax base — the welfare state
(Lesson 34) and public debt are claims on future productivity;
and the US gap is the emigration pull on Canada's most mobile
talent (Lesson 41). A rich country can coast on stock for a
generation; the gap is about the flow.
</details>

**Q3.** What is the branch-plant economy — origin, mechanism,
and one modern echo?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Origin: the National Policy's tariff wall (1879, Lesson 14)
induced US firms to open Canadian subsidiaries rather than
export over the wall — Canadian manufacturing grew as branch
operations of foreign head offices. Mechanism: decision rights,
R&D, and profits concentrate at the parent; the Canadian
operation optimizes for the Canadian market only, capping
scale and innovation locally. Modern echoes (any): world-class
Canadian AI research commercialized largely by US firms (and
Canadian tech talent working remotely for US payrolls —
Lesson 41); the thinness of Canadian-headquartered global
champions outside banking and infrastructure; recurring
foreign-takeover dramas over the few that emerge. The ghost
in the productivity file.
</details>
