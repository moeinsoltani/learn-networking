# Lesson 39: What Canada Makes and Sells

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **tidewater** — ocean access for shipping; landlocked Alberta's oil must reach "tidewater" to sell beyond the US.
> - **equity stake** — an ownership share in a project or company (some First Nations seek equity in pipelines).
> - **critical minerals** — the metals batteries and electronics need (lithium, nickel, cobalt, rare earths).
> - **LNG** — liquefied natural gas: gas cooled to liquid so ships can carry it overseas.
> - **cluster** — many firms of one industry concentrated in one city, feeding each other (Montreal aerospace).
> - **incumbent** — here: the established dominant company (BlackBerry was the incumbent the iPhone beat).
> - **memento mori** (meh-MEN-toh MOR-ee) — Latin: "remember you will die" — a cautionary reminder (Nortel and BlackBerry play this role).
> - **VFX** — visual effects for film; **CanCon** — Canada's rules requiring Canadian content in broadcasting (Lesson 46).
> - **TSX** — the Toronto Stock Exchange.
> - **duty to consult** — the Crown's obligation to consult Indigenous nations before projects on their lands (Lesson 24).
> - Names: **Saguenay** (sag-eh-NAY), **Bombardier** (bom-bar-dee-AY), **Bengio** (BEN-jee-oh), **Mila** (MEE-lah — Montreal's AI institute), **Kitimat** (KIT-ih-mat).

## Concept

Lesson 05 mapped the resources; this lesson maps the *industries* —
who employs, who exports, where the head offices sit, and which
files generate the political wars. The map, by flagship:

Canadian industry clusters geographically to a degree that surprises people
from larger countries — most sectors have one obvious capital, and knowing
which city goes with which industry explains a great deal of regional
politics.

| Sector | Where it lives |
|---|---|
| **Energy** | Oil sands, gas, and pipelines run out of **Calgary**; the hydro giants out of **Montreal**, **Vancouver**, and **St. John's**. This is Canada's number-one export |
| **Mining** | Potash and uranium in **Saskatoon**; nickel and gold headquartered in **Toronto**, which is the world's mining *finance* capital — the TSX lists more of the planet's miners than any other exchange; diamonds in **Yellowknife**; and critical minerals, the gold rush of the 2020s |
| **Agri-food** | Wheat and canola across the **Prairies**; supply-managed dairy in **Quebec and Ontario** (Lesson 38); beef in **Alberta**; seafood on both coasts |
| **Autos** | Ontario's **Windsor–Oshawa corridor** — assembly for the Detroit Three plus Toyota and Honda, and now the EV-battery bet |
| **Aerospace** | **Montreal** — Bombardier, CAE, Pratt & Whitney Canada; a genuinely top-tier global cluster |
| **Banking** | **Toronto** — the Big Five (Lesson 40) |
| **Tech** | The **Toronto–Waterloo corridor**, with its university pipeline; **Montreal** for AI research and video games; **Vancouver** for games, visual effects, and film |
| **Culture** | Film and TV production in **Toronto, Vancouver, and Montreal** — "Hollywood North" — plus games and music (Lesson 46's CanCon machinery) |

Plus the two cautionary tales every Canadian retells — **Nortel**
(at its 2000 peak ~a third of the TSX's entire value; bankrupt by
2009, its patents scattered) and **BlackBerry** (the smartphone's
inventor, out-innovated by 2012, surviving as a software
remnant) — the national memento mori: Canada *can* grow global
tech champions, and has twice watched them die, which shapes both
its investor caution and its Shopify-era determination not to
repeat the arc.

## Going Deeper

### Energy: the superpower with a plumbing problem

The oil sands (Lesson 05) make Canada the world's #4 oil producer
— landlocked in Alberta, a thousand-plus kilometres from tidewater
in any direction. Hence **the pipeline wars**, the defining
resource politics of the era, always a three-government collision
(Lesson 29's federalism at maximum temperature): **Northern
Gateway** (killed — BC coast opposition, Indigenous litigation);
**Energy East** (killed — Quebec opposition); **Keystone XL**
(killed — by *US* presidential permit politics, twice: the
elephant's twitch, Lesson 38); and **Trans Mountain (TMX)** — BC
vs Alberta open constitutional warfare, protests, court-ordered
re-consultations… resolved only when **Ottawa bought the pipeline
itself (2018, ~$4.5B; completed 2024, ~$34B)** — the federal
government nationalizing a pipeline it could not politically
permit anyone else to build: Lesson 14's CPR pattern (public
purpose, public purse, private incapacity), rerun verbatim. Each
project's fault lines: provincial jurisdiction vs federal
interprovincial power; the duty to consult and Indigenous title
(Lessons 17/24 — with nations on *both* sides: litigants against,
and equity-stake proponents for — Lesson 44); and climate policy
vs the export account (Lesson 37). Meanwhile hydro (Quebec, BC,
Manitoba) quietly exports clean power south, and the LNG buildout
(Kitimat) is the diversification bet of Lesson 38.

### Mining and the critical-minerals turn

Toronto finances the world's mining (TSX/TSXV list ~40% of global
mining equity — the Shield's vault, Lesson 02, become a financial
specialty); Saskatchewan feeds it (potash #1 globally, uranium
#2). The 2020s turn: **critical minerals** (lithium, cobalt,
nickel, rare earths) as Western supply-chain security policy —
Canada as the democratic world's designated mine, with the same
collision course as pipelines: the deposits sit on treaty and
title lands (the Ring of Fire in Ontario's north is the test
case), so the industry's future runs through Lesson 44's
consent economics.

### Manufacturing's two clusters

**Ontario autos**: ~one in five manufacturing jobs; the
integration story of Lesson 38 (parts crossing repeatedly, the
IRA scare, the EV-battery subsidy race — Stellantis/VW plants
landed with multi-billion incentives matching Washington's).
**Montreal aerospace**: one of the world's three great clusters
(with Seattle and Toulouse) — Bombardier's business jets, CAE's
flight simulators (global #1), engines, satellites; also the
premier example of state-nursed industry (decades of federal
support, the C-Series/Airbus saga — sold for one dollar under US
tariff assault, Lesson 38's asymmetry in a single transaction).

### Tech: the third attempt

Wave one, Nortel; wave two, BlackBerry; wave three: **Shopify**
(Ottawa — at times Canada's most valuable company, the
existence-proof that a Canadian software firm can stay
independent and global), the **Toronto–Waterloo corridor**
(North America's fastest-growing tech-talent pool through the
2010s–20s, fed by Waterloo's co-op engineering pipeline and
immigration policy — Lesson 41), and **Montreal AI** — Yoshua
Bengio's Mila, deep-learning's academic cradle (with Hinton in
Toronto: two of the field's three godfathers worked in Canada —
the research half of the story). The structural asterisks, from
Lesson 37: commercialization leaks south (invented here, scaled
there), talent arbitrage (US payrolls, remote or relocated —
Lesson 41's gap), and the venture-capital depth difference. The
national bet: immigration + research + anchor firms eventually
compound into retained champions. Verdict pending; the
cautionary tales sit on the shelf, watching.

{: .note }
> **"Hollywood North" and the games empire**
> Cultural industry is real industry: Toronto and Vancouver
> rank among North America's largest film/TV production
> centres (tax credits + talent + the dollar, playing every
> American city on screen), Montreal is a top-five global
> games hub (Ubisoft's largest studio; the 1997 Quebec
> multimedia tax credit is the textbook successful industrial
> policy nobody expected), and Canadian VFX touches half of
> Hollywood's output. File with Lesson 46: the CanCon
> machinery protects Canadian *content*; the production boom
> is the opposite play — exporting Canadian *capacity* into
> everyone else's content. Both worked; nobody planned the
> second.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* Name Canada's flagship industry in
each: Alberta, Saskatchewan, Ontario, Quebec, BC — plus the
financial specialization of Toronto.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Alberta — oil and gas (Calgary head offices, Fort McMurray
sands). Saskatchewan — potash (world #1) and uranium, atop the
grain economy. Ontario — auto manufacturing (Windsor–Oshawa,
now the EV-battery corridor) plus banking and tech. Quebec —
aerospace (Montreal's global cluster), hydro power, aluminum,
AI research, and the supply-managed dairy heartland. BC —
forestry, the Pacific trade gateway (port of Vancouver), film
production, and LNG. Toronto's specialization: mining finance —
the TSX lists roughly 40% of the world's mining equity — atop
its Big Five banking role (Lesson 40).
</details>

**Q2.** Why do pipelines produce Canada's fiercest resource
politics? Name the three governments and the two Indigenous
positions in a typical fight.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Because they cross every fault line at once: the producing
province (Alberta — provincial resource ownership, Lesson 05)
needs tidewater; transit/coastal provinces (BC, or Quebec for
Energy East) bear risk without proportionate benefit and
resist; Ottawa holds interprovincial jurisdiction (Lesson 29)
plus climate policy plus national unity — so every project is
a three-government collision. Indigenous positions genuinely
divide: some nations litigate against projects on
consultation, title, and environmental grounds (the court
victories that halted TMX and killed Northern Gateway), while
others negotiate equity ownership and benefit agreements as
economic reconciliation — both authentic, per Lesson 06's
rule. Add the elephant (US permits killed Keystone XL) and
the result: since 2010, roughly one export pipeline
completed — by the federal government buying it.
</details>

**Q3.** What do the Nortel and BlackBerry stories jointly teach,
and what is the third wave's structural answer?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Jointly: that Canada can create world-leading technology
champions (both were global #1s) and that a single-champion
ecosystem makes their individual failures — one of governance
in a bust, one of strategic misread in a platform shift —
national traumas: talent, capital, and confidence all
concentrated, all lost together. Structural answer of the
Toronto–Waterloo/Montreal third wave: portfolio over champion
— many mid-size firms plus one anchor (Shopify), fed by
deliberate talent depth (university co-op pipelines and
tech-targeted immigration, Lesson 41) so the ecosystem
survives any single death; plus earlier governance and the
unresolvable residue (Lesson 37): keeping the
commercialization and the cap tables in Canada, which no
policy has yet reliably achieved.
</details>
