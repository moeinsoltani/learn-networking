# Lesson 27: The Prime Minister and Cabinet

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

Start with the fact that surprises every newcomer: **nobody in Canada
votes for Prime Minister.** The PM is simply the person who commands
the House of Commons' confidence — an MP like any other on the ballot
(their name appears only in their own riding), whose national power is
entirely derivative of Lesson 12's rule. There is no PM line in the
constitution's 1867 text at all; the office exists by convention.

And yet, in practice, a **majority-government Canadian PM is among
the most powerful executives in the democratic world** — more
domestically unconstrained than a US president. Hold both truths:

Start with the oddity: **formally, the Prime Minister has nothing.** The office
is not created by the Constitution Act, 1867; it exists by convention, and the
constitution scarcely mentions it.

In practice the powers are enormous. The PM commands a whipped Commons
majority, which in a majority parliament means the legislature does what the
PM wants. The PM appoints the cabinet, the senators, the judges — including
the Supreme Court — the Governor General, the heads of Crown corporations, and
the ambassadors. The PM controls the government's agenda and the House's time,
and controls who may run as a candidate, since party nomination papers require
the leader's signature. The PM advises dissolution, and so effectively chooses
the election date, within the loose grip of the fixed-date law. And the PM
sits atop the Prime Minister's Office, the political nerve centre of the whole
government.

Three things brake all of that. The first is **confidence**: lose the House
and lose everything (Lesson 12). The second is **caucus and party** — leaders
who lose the room fall, and while Canadian caucuses are tamer than British
ones, they are not inert. The third is **federalism and the Charter**:
premiers and courts, which Lessons 29 and 31 take up.

The system's honest description: **elective near-dictatorship
between elections, on a confidence leash** — with minority
parliaments (frequent in Canada, Lesson 28's math) as the leash
pulled short.

## Going Deeper

### Forming a government

Election night produces seat counts (Lesson 28); then convention
takes over: **the incumbent PM stays PM until resignation or
dismissal** — even having "lost," they're entitled to meet the House
first (rarely exercised when the loss is clear; standard when the
result is hung). The GG (Lesson 25) calls on whoever can command
confidence — largest party *usually*, but a smaller party with
partners can govern (federal precedent: King in 1925 governed from
second place with Progressive support). **Coalitions**[°](#w-coalition) (opposition
parties in cabinet together) are constitutionally routine and
politically radioactive in Canada — attempted 2008 (Lesson 25's
prorogation), achieved federally only in wartime; the Canadian norm
is instead the **minority government**[°](#w-minority-government) (governing party alone,
surviving vote-by-vote) or, newer, the **supply-and-confidence
agreement** (a smaller party pledges confidence-vote support for
policy concessions — the 2022–24 Liberal–NDP arrangement being the
modern template).

### Cabinet: the government, plural

The PM selects ministers (MPs almost always; a senator
occasionally; a non-parliamentarian must win a seat fast, by
convention). The cabinet-building constraints are famously Canadian:
**every province represented if caucus allows; French and English;
gender parity since 2015 ("because it's 2015" — Trudeau's answer);
urban/rural; and increasingly Indigenous and visible-minority
representation** — Canadian cabinets are demographic maps first,
talent allocations second (both, on good days). Ministers head
departments (Finance, Global Affairs, Health...), swear the Privy
Council oath, and live by two conventions:

- **Cabinet solidarity:** argue privately, defend publicly, or
  resign — the government speaks with one voice (a resigning
  minister's speech is Ottawa's highest drama: Chrétien's
  ministers over Meech, Jody Wilson-Raybould in 2019).
- **Ministerial responsibility:** the minister answers to the
  House for the department — the constitutional fiction that keeps
  a name attached to every file (in decay everywhere — modern
  ministers rarely resign for departmental failure — but still the
  frame of Question Period, Lesson 26).

**Finance** is first among equals (the budget is the confidence
centrepiece and the PM–Finance relationship is every government's
plotline: Chrétien–Martin the canonical feud); the **Treasury
Board** controls spending authority; and above the departments

### The PMO and "presidentialization"

The **Prime Minister's Office** — partisan staff, unelected,
answerable only to the PM (chief of staff, comms, issues
management) — has grown since the 1970s into the government's true
command centre: message discipline, appointment vetting, minister
"**mandate letters**[°](#w-mandate-letter)" (public since 2015 — the PM's written
instructions to each minister; read one and you know who works for
whom). Alongside: the **Privy Council Office** (the *non-partisan*
civil-service twin — cabinet's secretariat, the deputy ministers'
apex). Critics from Savoie (**"court government"**[°](#w-court-government)) onward argue
Canadian PMs govern more centrally than presidents — no separated
legislature, no primaries, no independent cabinet barons — and
every recent PM has promised cabinet government's restoration and
then centralized further (the incentives never change). The
counterweights that actually bite: minority math, caucus revolts
(rarer federally than provincially, but the Reform-descended
parties gave caucuses formal removal tools via the Reform Act —
used provincially, brandished federally), premiers (Lesson 29),
courts (Lesson 31), and the four-year clock.

### How governments end

Four exits: **defeat at a general election** (the usual);
**losing a confidence vote** (six federal governments — most
recently 2011; typically minority governments, triggering
elections); **resignation mid-term** (party leadership change:
the new leader becomes PM unelected-as-such — Kim Campbell,
John Turner — perfectly constitutional, invariably attacked as
illegitimate, usually short-lived); and never, so far, **dismissal
by the Crown** (the loaded-and-never-fired reserve power,
Lesson 25). Fixed-date election laws (since 2007) set October
dates every four years — but expressly preserve the GG's
dissolution power, so PMs still call **snap elections**[°](#w-snap-election) when polls
tempt (2008, 2021: both called early by the law's own author-
governments; both returned minorities — the electorate's opinion
of opportunism is its own check).

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* How does someone become Prime Minister
of Canada, and how can a government fall between elections?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
By commanding the confidence of the House of Commons — normally as
leader of the party that can assemble a working majority after an
election (or mid-term, by winning the governing party's
leadership). No one is elected PM directly; the GG appoints
whoever can hold the House. A government falls between elections
by losing a confidence vote — the budget, explicit no-confidence
motions, or measures the government declares confidence in —
after which the PM either advises dissolution (an election) or
resigns in favour of an alternative that can command the House
(GG's judgment in the rare unclear cases — Lessons 12, 25).
</details>

**Q2.** List five significant appointment powers held in practice by
the PM, and name the three real constraints on prime-ministerial
power.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Appointments (any five): cabinet ministers; senators (via advice,
now filtered through the advisory board); Supreme Court and
superior-court judges; the Governor General; deputy ministers;
heads of Crown corporations and agencies; ambassadors. Constraints:
(1) confidence — the House can end the government any sitting day,
sharpest in minorities; (2) party and caucus — leaders who lose
their party fall without elections (leadership reviews, caucus
revolt; the Reform Act mechanics); (3) the constitutional
counterweights — federalism (premiers control half the state,
Lesson 29) and the Charter/courts (Lesson 31), plus the practical
fourth: the electorate's four-year audit, which punishes visible
arrogance (snap-election backfires of 2008 and 2021).
</details>

**Q3.** What are cabinet solidarity and ministerial responsibility,
and why does the system need each?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Solidarity: ministers may fight freely in the cabinet room but
must publicly support every cabinet decision or resign — needed
because a government that argues with itself in public cannot hold
confidence, negotiate, or be accountable as a unit (the House must
be able to fire <em>a government</em>, not adjudicate its internal
factions). Responsibility: each minister answers personally to
the House for their department's conduct — needed to keep a
nameable human attached to every exercise of state power (the
alternative is "the system did it"). Both conventions are fraying
— message discipline has moved solidarity's enforcement to the
PMO, and resignation-for-departmental-failure is nearly extinct —
but they remain the grammar of accountability: Question Period,
scandals, and resignations only make sense inside them.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-incumbent"></a>**incumbent** (in-KUM-bent) — the current holder of an office.
- <a id="w-minority-government"></a>**minority government** — a government whose party holds under half the seats, surviving vote by vote; <a id="w-coalition"></a>**coalition** — two+ parties actually sharing cabinet (rare in Canada).
- <a id="w-supply-and-confidence-agreement"></a>**supply-and-confidence agreement** — a smaller party promises to support the government on confidence votes in exchange for policy wins ("supply" = approving spending).
- <a id="w-cabinet-solidarity"></a>**cabinet solidarity** — ministers must publicly support every cabinet decision or resign; <a id="w-ministerial-responsibility"></a>**ministerial responsibility** — each minister answers to Parliament for their department.
- <a id="w-pmo"></a>**PMO** — the Prime Minister's Office: the PM's *political* staff; <a id="w-pco"></a>**PCO** — the Privy Council Office: the *non-partisan* civil-service counterpart.
- <a id="w-mandate-letter"></a>**mandate letter** — the PM's published instruction letter telling each minister what to deliver.
- <a id="w-snap-election"></a>**snap election** — an election called early, by surprise, when polls look good.
- <a id="w-court-government"></a>**"court government"** — scholar Donald Savoie's (sav-WAH) phrase for power concentrated in the PM and unelected advisers, like a royal court.
- <a id="w-shuffle"></a>**shuffle** — the PM reassigning ministers to new jobs.
- <a id="w-lame-duck"></a>**lame duck** — an office-holder whose power is fading because their exit is known.
- Names: **Wilson-Raybould** (WIL-sun RAY-boold), **Kim Campbell**, **Meighen** (MEE-en), **24 Sussex** (SUSS-ex — the PM's official residence).
