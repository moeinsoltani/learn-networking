# Lesson 29: Federalism — Who Does What

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

The single most useful civic skill in Canada: **knowing which
government to blame.** Canada is among the world's most **decentralized**[°](#w-decentralized)
federations — the provinces are not administrative districts but
constitutional sovereigns within their own list — and the division
written in 1867 (Lesson 13's ss. 91–92) still allocates the files:

The single most useful thing to internalize is which level does what, because
newcomers routinely write to the wrong government. The surprise for most
people is that **health care and education — the two biggest things
governments do — are provincial**, not federal. Ottawa does defence, foreign
affairs, criminal law, money, and the border; the provinces do almost
everything you actually touch.

| Level | Main responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Federal** (s.91) | Defence and foreign affairs; citizenship and immigration (shared); criminal **law** — one Criminal Code for the whole country; money and banking; trade and tariffs; Employment Insurance and the Canada Pension Plan; "Indians and lands reserved for the Indians" (Lesson 17 — the file is now called Crown–Indigenous Relations); the post, the census, and fisheries |
| **Provincial** (ss. 92, 92A, 93) | **Health care** and **education** at all levels; "property and civil rights," which means most of private law — contracts, labour, housing, professions, insurance; natural resources (Lesson 05); municipalities; the administration of justice — police, courts, and prisons for sentences under two years; highways, electricity, and liquor |
| **Shared or contested** | Immigration (**concurrent**[°](#w-concurrent) in the text itself), agriculture, the environment and carbon pricing (allocated mostly by judges, case by case), and housing — which is everyone's and no one's |
| **Municipal** | Exists only because a provincial statute says so: potholes, zoning, transit, garbage. A big city's powers are whatever its province permits this year |
| **Territorial** | Powers delegated federally, evolving steadily toward province-like devolution (Lesson 01) |

Sit with that first surprise for a second: the Prime Minister you
watched debating health care on national television runs exactly
zero hospitals. The system's glue and battleground is **money**: Ottawa
raises more than its files cost, provinces' files cost more than
they raise — and the transfers that bridge the gap (Canada Health
Transfer, equalization — Lesson 35) are where federalism's real
politics happens.

## Going Deeper

### Why the map looks like this

1867's drafters (Lesson 13) gave the "great" matters of the era —
railways, banks, war, criminal law — to Ottawa, and the "local and
private" matters — schools, hospitals (then church charities),
property — to provinces, expecting a centralized federation.
History inverted the weights: the local list turned out to contain
the welfare state (health, education, social services — trivial in
1867, half the economy now), and judicial interpretation (the
Privy Council's decades of province-friendly rulings on "property
and civil rights") plus Quebec's existential stake in provincial
power (the whole point of the bargain) made Canada progressively
more decentralized as the U.S. centralized. Add s.92A's resource
ownership (Lesson 05) and provinces control most of what touches
daily life.

### The federal levers that blur the map

- **The spending power** (Lesson 21's medicare mechanism): Ottawa
  can't legislate in provincial fields but can attach conditions
  to money — hence "national" programs that are really federal
  cheques + provincial delivery + negotiated conditions (medicare
  via the Canada Health Act, child care via bilateral deals,
  housing accelerator funds...). Quebec's standing objection and
  frequent opt-out-with-compensation is the system's asymmetric
  release valve (QPP, immigration selection, child care précédent).
- **Criminal law power**: one federal Criminal Code (unlike the US
  patchwork) — but provinces administer justice and run the
  police/prosecutions, so "federal law, provincial enforcement" is
  the norm (cannabis legalization: federal act, thirteen different
  retail regimes — the perfect teaching example).
- **POGG**[°](#w-pogg) (Lesson 13's phrase): the residual power occasionally
  producing national **jurisdiction**[°](#w-jurisdiction) — most recently the Supreme
  Court's 2021 carbon-pricing reference: climate's "minimum
  national standards" qualified as a matter of national concern —
  federal **backstop**[°](#w-backstop) upheld, provincial fury undiminished
  (Lesson 39).
- **Declaratory & emergency powers**: mostly dormant relics —
  except the Emergencies Act's 2022 debut (Lesson 20).

### The intergovernmental game

No constitutional organ coordinates the federation (the
constitution barely mentions how governments should relate);
instead: **First Ministers' Meetings** (PM + premiers — held when
the PM chooses, which premiers perennially protest), the **Council
of the Federation** (premiers alone, issuing communiqués Ottawa
files carefully in the bin), and thousands of quiet
official-level agreements. The recurring dramas run on two axes:
**money** (transfers, equalization — the West's and Alberta's
file, Lesson 35) and **jurisdiction** (carbon pricing, pipelines,
health strings, immigration levels). And one structural absurdity
every newcomer eventually discovers: **internal trade barriers**[°](#w-internal-trade-barriers) —
provincial regulatory fences (trucking rules, professional
licensing, alcohol distribution) that can make it easier to import
wine from France than from the next province; economists price the
drag in the tens of billions annually; reform proceeds at the
speed of thirteen governments each protecting something.

### Municipalities: the missing tier

Cities — where 80%+ of Canadians live (Lesson 04) — have **no
constitutional existence**: they are "creatures of the provinces,"
exercising delegated powers a province can amend overnight
(Ontario halved Toronto's council mid-election in 2018; the courts
shrugged — provincial power is that **plenary**[°](#w-plenary)). Consequence: the
governments closest to daily life (transit, housing supply,
policing) have the narrowest tax base (property tax + fees) and
the least constitutional protection — which is why every big-city
mayor's speech is a plea toward two senior governments, and why
"housing crisis" files bounce among three levels each holding one
piece (federal: immigration volume + financing; provincial:
zoning frameworks + tenant law; municipal: permits + **NIMBY**[°](#w-nimby)
politics). When Canadians seem confused about who owes them a
solution, they are accurately perceiving the design.

{: .note }
> **The blame-routing table (clip and save)**
> Passport, EI, CRA/taxes-federal-portion, citizenship, defence,
> First Nations services, interest rates* → **Ottawa** (*Bank of
> Canada, **arm's length**[°](#w-arms-length)). Hospital waits, school curriculum,
> driver's licence, tenant rights, minimum wage (most jobs),
> family law process, liquor rules, long-term care → **your
> province**. Potholes, transit frequency, zoning, garbage,
> parks, local police board → **your city (via your province)**.
> Housing prices → all three plus the Bank, which is why that
> file never resolves (Lesson 40). First test of Canadian civic
> literacy: the PM cannot fix your emergency room, and your
> premier cannot fix your passport office.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* Name four exclusively federal and four
exclusively provincial responsibilities — including the two big
ones everyone mis-assigns.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Federal: defence, foreign affairs, citizenship, criminal law,
currency/banking, EI, fisheries, the post. Provincial: health
care and education (the two everyone assigns to Ottawa), plus
property and civil rights (most private law), natural resources,
municipalities, highways, administration of justice. Shared or
contested: immigration (concurrent, federal paramountcy),
agriculture, environment. Municipalities exercise only powers
delegated by their province.
</details>

**Q2.** What is the federal spending power, why is it
constitutionally controversial, and give two programs built on it.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Ottawa's practice of spending (and attaching conditions) in
fields it cannot legislate — the workaround by which a
decentralized constitution hosts "national" social programs.
Controversial because it purchases jurisdiction the constitution
withheld: provinces (Quebec doctrinally, all provinces when
convenient) call it backdoor centralization that distorts their
priorities and then underfunds the obligations it created
(the eternal health-transfer fight). Programs: medicare (Canada
Health Act conditions on the Canada Health Transfer), $10-a-day
child care (bilateral agreements), plus housing funds, and
historically the CPP's twin (with Quebec opting out into the QPP
— the asymmetry release valve in action).
</details>

**Q3.** Why is the PM blamed for hospitals he doesn't run — and
what does the answer reveal about federalism's chief cost?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Because visibility and responsibility are misaligned by design:
Ottawa raises marquee revenue and sets marquee narratives
(national campaigns, the biggest megaphone), while provinces
deliver the services that hurt when they fail; voters route
anger by salience, not by ss. 91–92. Each level exploits this —
Ottawa announces "national" programs it won't deliver, provinces
blame federal underfunding for outcomes they administer,
municipalities blame both — producing federalism's chief cost:
<strong>accountability fog</strong>. The offsetting benefit,
which Canada consistently chooses (Lessons 13, 22, 23): the
same jurisdictional depth that blurs blame is what lets four
nations, six regions, and thirteen governments share one state
without constant existential collision. Clear accountability
and deep pluralism trade off; Canada bought pluralism.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-jurisdiction"></a>**jurisdiction** (joor-iss-DIK-shun) — the legal authority to make laws about a subject; the core word of this lesson.
- <a id="w-decentralized"></a>**decentralized** — with power spread out to the provinces rather than concentrated in Ottawa.
- <a id="w-concurrent"></a>**concurrent** (kun-KUR-ent) — a field both levels may legislate in (immigration, agriculture); <a id="w-paramountcy"></a>**paramountcy** (pair-ah-MOWNT-see) — the rule that federal law wins when the two conflict.
- <a id="w-transfer-payments"></a>**transfer payments** — money Ottawa sends to provinces (the Canada Health Transfer, equalization).
- <a id="w-spending-power"></a>**spending power** — Ottawa's ability to attach conditions to money even in provincial fields (Lesson 21).
- <a id="w-pogg"></a>**POGG** — "Peace, Order, and good Government," the federal residual power (Lesson 13); a <a id="w-backstop"></a>**backstop** — a federal rule that applies only where a province has no equivalent.
- <a id="w-creatures-of-the-province"></a>**"creatures of the province"** — the legal status of cities: they exist only by provincial law; <a id="w-plenary"></a>**plenary** (PLEN-ah-ree) — complete, unrestricted (provincial power over cities is plenary).
- <a id="w-first-ministers"></a>**First Ministers** — the PM and the provincial premiers, collectively.
- <a id="w-internal-trade-barriers"></a>**internal trade barriers** — provincial rules that block goods/workers from other provinces.
- <a id="w-nimby"></a>**NIMBY** (NIM-bee) — "Not In My Back Yard": residents supporting something in principle but opposing it near them.
- <a id="w-arms-length"></a>**arm's length** — operating independently of political control (the Bank of Canada).
