# Lesson 24: Reconciliation and the Canada of Today

{: .note }
> **Words to know** *(simple definitions + how to say the names)*
> - **reconciliation** (rek-un-sil-ee-AY-shun) — repairing the relationship between Canada and Indigenous peoples: truth first, then redress and structural change.
> - **White Paper** — a government document proposing a policy (the famous 1969 one proposed abolishing Indian status).
> - **Aboriginal title** — Indigenous nations' legal ownership-type right to their traditional lands; **duty to consult** — the Crown's obligation to consult nations before acting on their lands.
> - **class action** — one lawsuit brought on behalf of thousands of people harmed the same way.
> - **UNDRIP** (UN-drip) — the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.
> - **MMIWG** — Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (the national crisis and the 2019 inquiry into it).
> - **land acknowledgement** — the practice of naming, at the start of an event, whose traditional territory you are on.
> - **visible minority** — Statistics Canada's term for people who are non-white and non-Indigenous.
> - **denialism** — refusing to accept well-documented historical facts.
> - **ground-penetrating radar** — technology that detects disturbed soil (possible graves) without digging.
> - **special interlocutor** (in-ter-LOK-yoo-ter) — an official appointed to investigate an issue and recommend action.
> - Names: **Calder** (KAWL-der), **Nisga'a** (NISS-gah), **Tsilhqot'in** (sil-KOH-teen), **Delgamuukw** (del-gah-MOOK), **Oka** (OH-kah), **Kamloops** (KAM-loops), **Mary Simon** — first Indigenous Governor General, an Inuk (EE-nook) from northern Quebec.

## Concept

Phase 4 ends by bringing two arcs to the present. The first is the
country's reckoning with Lesson 17's machinery — **residential schools,
the Indian Act, the broken treaties** — a reckoning with a precise
public timeline:

The reckoning has a timeline, and reading it in order shows something
important: almost every step was forced from outside government, by Indigenous
organizing or by the courts, rather than offered from within.

| Year | What happened |
|---|---|
| **1969** | The **White Paper** proposes abolishing Indian status altogether. Indigenous opposition — the "Red Paper" — kills it, and in doing so *creates* the modern national political movement |
| **1973–** | The **Calder** case establishes that Aboriginal title exists in Canadian law, opening the era of modern treaties (James Bay 1975; Nisga'a 2000) |
| **1990** | Elijah Harper blocks Meech Lake (Lesson 23); the **Oka Crisis** — a 78-day armed standoff over a golf-course expansion onto Mohawk burial ground |
| **1996** | The **last residential school closes** (Gordon's, Saskatchewan); the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples reports |
| **1999** | **Nunavut** is created — an Inuit-majority territory |
| **2006** | The **Residential Schools Settlement**, the largest class action in Canadian history |
| **2008** | Prime Minister Harper's **apology** in the House of Commons; the Truth and Reconciliation Commission is created |
| **2015** | The **TRC final report**: the finding of "cultural genocide," and **94 Calls to Action** |
| **2016** | Canada adopts **UNDRIP**, the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (given legal force in 2021) |
| **2019** | The **MMIWG inquiry** reports on missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls, with a finding of genocide |
| **2021** | Ground-penetrating radar indicates 215 unmarked graves at **Kamloops** — a national convulsion. September 30 becomes the **National Day for Truth and Reconciliation** (Orange Shirt Day); **Mary Simon** becomes the first Indigenous Governor General |
| **2022** | **Pope Francis** travels to Canada and apologizes for the Church's role in the schools |

The second arc is demographic: the Canada doing this reckoning is no
longer Lesson 21's Canada. **Almost one in four Canadians is
foreign-born** (the G7's highest share); the largest source countries
are India, China, and the Philippines; Indigenous people are the
youngest, fastest-growing population; and the 40-million milestone
passed in 2023. The country examining its founding is simultaneously
being re-founded — by immigration — every decade. Whether those two
facts help or hinder each other is one of today's genuinely open
questions.

## Going Deeper

### From White Paper to Calls to Action

The modern era opens with a failed erasure: Trudeau's **1969 White
Paper** proposed ending Indian status, the Act, the treaties —
formal equality as final assimilation (Lesson 17's enfranchisement
logic, universalized). Indigenous response — Harold Cardinal's
*Citizens Plus* ("Red Paper") — rejected it so completely that the
movement it mobilized became the permanent political force behind
everything since: the **Calder** breakthrough (1973 — the Supreme
Court accepting that Aboriginal title *exists* in Canadian law,
reviving Lesson 10's Proclamation doctrine), the s.35 victory
(Lesson 23), *Delgamuukw* (oral history as evidence, Lesson 06),
*Tsilhqot'in* (2014 — first declared title land), the modern-treaty
era (James Bay 1975 → Nisga'a 2000 → ~25 comprehensive agreements
covering over 40% of Canada's land mass), and **Nunavut** (1999) —
Lesson 01's newest territory as the largest Indigenous land
settlement in history.

The schools' reckoning ran parallel: survivors' litigation forced
the **2006 Settlement Agreement** (compensation + a commission);
the **2008 apology** (Harper: "there is no place in Canada for the
attitudes that inspired the residential school system to ever again
prevail"); the **TRC** (2008–15) heard ~6,750 witnesses, found
**cultural genocide**, and issued **94 Calls to Action** — a public,
numbered, checkable to-do list (child welfare, language rights,
health, justice, a papal apology, September 30 itself...). Status
honestly: progress is real but slow — independent trackers count
well under a fifth "complete" a decade on; the structural items
(child welfare funding, water, the Act itself) move slowest. The
**2021 Kamloops** ground-radar findings (and those that followed at
other school sites) turned the TRC's documented history into a
national emotional event: flags at half-mast for months, orange
shirts everywhere, statues down — and a backlash discourse
("denialism" entered the Canadian vocabulary). September 30 now
institutionalizes the memory annually (Lesson 18
principle: the asymmetric calendar, hardest case).

### The present tense, both columns

**Column A — transformation underway:** Indigenous peoples as the
fastest-growing, youngest population (1.8M+, median age ~8 years
below national); language revitalization (Indigenous Languages Act
2019; Inuktitut in Nunavut's government); an economic surge
(development corporations, equity stakes in major projects,
Indigenous tourism, procurement targets); cultural ascendancy
(Kent Monkman in the Met's lobby, Tanya Tagaq's Polaris Prize,
Michelle Good and Richard Wagamese on every syllabus); Mary Simon
as GG; jurisprudence steadily converting Lesson 17's "live
documents" into enforced obligations (the 2024 Robinson annuities
ruling; child-welfare compensation orders in the tens of
billions). **Column B — the unfinished:** dozens of long-term
drinking-water advisories persisted into the 2020s (the target
date blown repeatedly); Indigenous people are ~5% of the
population and ~30%+ of the federally incarcerated; the MMIWG
crisis (the 2019 inquiry's finding: genocide); child-welfare
apprehension rates exceeding residential schools' at their peak;
suicide epidemics in northern communities; the Indian Act — still
in force (Lesson 17's cage, still locked around what it holds).
Reconciliation's honest status report: **a country that has
stopped denying, started paying, and not yet finished changing.**

### The demographic re-founding

Today's Canada, by the numbers that matter for everything after
this lesson: ~41 million (2024); ~23% foreign-born (rising);
visible minorities ~27% and majority in Toronto and Vancouver;
top source countries India, China, Philippines (the 1967 points
system + Lesson 43's streams doing exactly what they were designed
to do); French mother tongue ~21% and falling as a national share
(the anxiety inside every Quebec language debate, Lesson 22);
religious "nones" the largest single bloc — the Quiet Revolution's
secularization gone national. The 2020s stress test: a
record-setting immigration surge (population growing ~3%/year at
peak — developed-world extraordinary) colliding with Lesson 04's
housing funnel, producing the first sustained public turn against
high immigration targets in decades — and the 2024–25 recalibration
downward. The consensus Lesson 43 will examine — "immigration is
what Canada is" — is intact but no longer unexamined.

{: .note }
> **"Should I feel guilty?" — the standard newcomer's question**
> You'll meet this lesson's history at citizenship class, at
> September 30 assemblies, in land acknowledgements — and if you
> arrived last decade, none of it was done in your name. The
> serviceable distinction: **guilt attaches to acts; responsibility
> attaches to membership.** Joining Canada means joining a going
> concern — its treaties (you are now a treaty person, Lesson 17),
> its debts, its apologies, and its unfinished list, exactly as a
> new employee inherits the company's contracts without having
> signed them. What's asked of citizens — old and new — is not
> feeling bad but knowing the file and backing the repairs. This
> course is, in part, that file.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* What is September 30, what does the
orange shirt signify, and what national process created the day?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
The National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (statutory federally
since 2021), honouring residential-school survivors, victims, and
their communities. The orange shirt comes from survivor Phyllis
Webstad's account — the new orange shirt her grandmother bought
her, confiscated on her first day at a residential school (1973) —
which grew into grassroots Orange Shirt Day ("Every Child
Matters") from 2013. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's
Call to Action #80 recommended the statutory day; Parliament
enacted it weeks after the 2021 Kamloops unmarked-graves findings.
</details>

**Q2.** Trace the legal arc from the 1969 White Paper to
Tsilhqot'in (2014) in four steps, naming what each step
established.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
(1) White Paper 1969 → its defeat by united Indigenous opposition
established that termination-as-equality was politically dead and
created the modern national organizations. (2) Calder 1973 → the
Supreme Court recognized Aboriginal title as a live legal doctrine
(reviving the Royal Proclamation's line, Lesson 10), prompting
Canada to open modern-treaty ("comprehensive claims")
negotiations — James Bay (1975) first. (3) s.35 (1982) →
constitutionalized existing Aboriginal and treaty rights, giving
the doctrine entrenchment (Lesson 23), with Sparrow, Delgamuukw
(oral evidence; title's content) and Haida (duty to consult)
building the framework. (4) Tsilhqot'in 2014 → first judicial
declaration of Aboriginal title over specific territory,
confirming title includes control (consent, not just
consultation) over the land. Net: from "the Act and status should
disappear" to "the nations hold constitutional, enforceable,
territorial rights" in one lifetime — with implementation, as
ever, trailing doctrine.
</details>

**Q3.** Give three statistics that define today's Canada
demographically, and one tension the combination produces.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Core set: population ~41 million (40M passed in 2023); roughly one
in four residents foreign-born — the G7's highest, with India,
China, and the Philippines the leading sources; visible minorities
~27% nationally (majorities in Toronto and Vancouver); Indigenous
population 1.8M+, the youngest and fastest-growing; French mother
tongue ~21% nationally and declining as a share. Tensions (any):
record immigration meeting Lesson 04's housing funnel produced
the 2020s affordability backlash and target cuts; French's
shrinking national weight sharpens Quebec's language politics
(Lesson 22); a re-founding-by-immigration country simultaneously
reckoning with its original founding creates the civic question
of Lesson 43 — how newcomers inherit both the pride and the file.
</details>
