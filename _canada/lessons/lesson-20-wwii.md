# Lesson 20: The Second World War

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

On **September 10, 1939** — one week after Britain — Canada declared war
on Germany by vote of its own Parliament. The week *was* the message
(Lesson 19's Statute of Westminster, exercised): Canada went to war as an
ally, not an appendage. What followed was total mobilization: from a
country of 11 million, **1.1 million served**; the war ended with Canada
holding the world's third-largest navy, fourth-largest air force — and
~45,000 dead.

The war has two halves that have to be held together, because Canadians tend
to remember one and forget the other.

**Abroad**, the longest campaign was the **Battle of the Atlantic** — the
navy's war of **convoy**[°](#w-convoy) escort against the **U-boats**[°](#w-u-boat), fought for nearly six years.
Then the land battles, in an order worth knowing: **Hong Kong** in 1941, where
some 2,000 Canadians were sent and all were lost, killed or taken prisoner;
**Dieppe** in 1942, the disaster that cost 907 dead in a single morning;
**Italy** from 1943, through Sicily and the house-to-house fighting at Ortona,
"little Stalingrad"; **D-Day**, June 6, 1944, where one of the five beaches —
**Juno** — was Canada's alone; and the **liberation of the Netherlands** in
1944–45, which is why Ottawa still receives Dutch tulips every spring. And
conscription returned, in Mackenzie King's famously contorted formula: "not
necessarily conscription, but conscription if necessary."

**At home**, the war built a modern industrial economy — from shells to
Spitfires — brought women into industry in large numbers (the "Bren Gun Girl"
was a national poster), and ran the British Commonwealth Air Training Plan,
which trained more than 130,000 aircrew and earned Canada the nickname "the
aerodrome of democracy." It also produced two acts the country later
apologized for. In 1942, roughly **22,000 Japanese Canadians** were removed
from the BC coast and their property confiscated and sold — apology and
**redress**[°](#w-redress) came in 1988. And in 1939 the **MS St. Louis**, carrying Jewish
refugees, was turned away under the policy remembered by the phrase "None is
too many" — apology in 2018. By the war's end the welfare state had begun
(family allowance, the veterans' charter) and Canada was a founding member of
the United Nations.

Hold the doubled ledger as usual — but note this war's particular
structure: **maximum honour abroad, two of the country's worst moral
failures at home**, and both now formally apologized for. This lesson is
where modern Canada's self-image — liberator, peacekeeper-to-be — and
its habit of late apology were both minted.

## Going Deeper

### The war abroad, campaign by campaign

- **The Atlantic (1939–45):** Canada's longest battle — the RCN grew
  from 13 ships to ~400, escorting the convoys that kept Britain alive;
  Halifax was the convoy gateway (and in 1917 had already paid the
  war-port price with the Halifax Explosion — the largest pre-atomic
  man-made blast, ~2,000 dead; file for connections). U-boats sank
  ships within sight of the St. Lawrence shore.
- **Hong Kong (December 1941):** 1,975 Canadians sent to an
  indefensible **garrison**[°](#w-garrison) weeks before Japan attacked; all were killed or
  captured — the POWs enduring years in camps at ~30% death rates. The
  war's first Canadian land battle, and its bitterest "why."
- **Dieppe (August 19, 1942):** the raid on the French port — ~5,000
  Canadians in the main force; in one morning 907 killed, ~1,900
  captured. Defenders of the raid say its lessons (never assault a
  port; need for overwhelming fire support and specialized armour)
  shaped D-Day's success; critics call that retrospective varnish on a
  bungle. Both live in Canadian memory — Dieppe is the country's
  Gallipoli.
- **Italy (1943–45):** Sicily, then the peninsula — including
  **Ortona** (Christmas 1943), vicious house-to-house fighting the
  press called "little Stalingrad." ~93,000 Canadians served in Italy;
  it became "the forgotten campaign" once Normandy started.
- **D-Day and Normandy (June 6, 1944):** of the five invasion beaches,
  **Juno** was assigned to Canada — 14,000 ashore on the day, deeper
  penetration than any other beach by nightfall, at 1,074 casualties
  (359 dead). Through the brutal Norman summer (Caen, Falaise) the
  First Canadian Army — the largest formation ever commanded by a
  Canadian — fought as one of the campaign's three great armies.
- **The Scheldt and the Netherlands (1944–45):** Canada's signature
  campaign — clearing the flooded Scheldt **estuary**[°](#w-estuary) to open Antwerp
  (miserable, half-forgotten, decisive), then liberating the
  Netherlands through the Hunger Winter, ending the war as the Dutch
  liberators. The gratitude is institutional: the Dutch royal family
  had sheltered in Ottawa (Princess Margriet was born there — the
  maternity ward declared **extraterritorial**[°](#w-extraterritorial) so she'd be born "in the
  Netherlands"), and the Netherlands sends **100,000+ tulip bulbs
  every year** — Ottawa's Canadian Tulip Festival is the war's
  loveliest receipt.

### King's tightrope: conscription without a crisis (almost)

PM **Mackenzie King** — Laurier's heir, holder of the country's
longest tenure, a man whose diaries reveal séances with his dead
mother and dog, and the century's shrewdest Canadian politician —
governed the war with one fixed star: **no repeat of 1917**
(Lesson 18). His method was calculated ambiguity, peak-form in the
era's most famous Canadian sentence: *"Not necessarily conscription,
but conscription if necessary."* The sequence: overseas conscription
ruled out (1939) → national **plebiscite**[°](#w-plebiscite) (1942) asking release from that
promise — **English Canada 80% yes, Quebec 73% no** (the survey says
everything) → power taken but shelved → finally invoked November 1944
after infantry losses; ~13,000 conscripts sent, ~2,500 reached the
front. Quebec grumbled, a minister resigned, one brief mutiny at
Terrace, BC — and the country held. Compare 1917's riots and dead:
King's fudge, much mocked, was statecraft. The lesson Canadian
politics took: **on existential French–English questions, ambiguity
is not weakness but load-bearing structure** — remember this at
Lesson 22's referendums.

### The home front's two great shames

- **Japanese Canadian internment (1942–49):** weeks after Pearl
  Harbor, under the **War Measures Act**[°](#w-war-measures-act), ~22,000 Japanese Canadians —
  **75% of them British subjects/Canadian-born** — were removed from
  a 100-mile BC coastal zone to camps and farms inland. Distinct from
  the U.S. version, worse in two respects: their **property was
  confiscated and sold** (boats, farms, Vancouver's Japantown) to pay
  for their own internment, and exclusion from the coast lasted until
  **1949** — four years past the war. The RCMP and military had
  advised it was unnecessary; politics (BC's decades of anti-Asian
  agitation, Lesson 16) demanded it. Redress: 1988 — formal apology
  (Mulroney), $21,000 per survivor, and the citizenship of the
  deported restored posthumously.
- **The *St. Louis* and "None is too many" (1939):** the liner
  carrying 907 German Jewish refugees, turned away by Cuba and the
  U.S., asked Canada last; Ottawa refused (an immigration official,
  asked how many Jews Canada should admit, had already supplied the
  era's epitaph: "None is too many"). The ship returned to Europe;
  254 of its passengers died in **the Holocaust**[°](#w-the-holocaust). Canada admitted fewer
  Jewish refugees per capita than nearly any Western state, 1933–45.
  Formal apology: 2018 (Trudeau). When Lesson 43 celebrates Canada's
  refugee tradition, this is its "before" photo — the tradition is a
  post-1945 construction, built partly in shame at this record.

### The war as state-builder

The war ended the Depression in eighteen months and built the modern
Canadian state: unemployment insurance (1940), **family allowances
(1945 — the first universal program, cheques to every mother)**, the
Veterans Charter (free university for veterans — a generation of
engineers and teachers), a taxation architecture centralized in
Ottawa (the wartime "tax rental" agreements — the fiscal plumbing
behind Lesson 29/35's federal spending power), C.D. Howe's crown
corporations, and a foreign-policy identity: founding member of the
UN (1945), of NATO (1949 — a Canadian push, the "Canadian article"
Article 2 on economic cooperation), with Lesson 21's peacekeeping
invention already loading. Population 11 million; seat at every
table. The war is when "**middle power**[°](#w-middle-power)" — Canada's entire diplomatic
brand — was coined and earned.

{: .note }
> **September 10, not September 3**
> The one-week delay is the most eloquent single week in Canadian
> constitutional history. 1914: at war by London's declaration,
> automatically (Lesson 18). 1939: Parliament debated, voted, and
> Canada declared itself — the week existing solely to prove the
> choice was real. (King even used the gap to import American
> aircraft legally while the U.S. Neutrality Acts still allowed
> shipment to a non-belligerent — sovereignty with a side of
> procurement.) When a citizenship-test question asks what the
> Statute of Westminster meant, "September 10, 1939" is the concrete
> answer.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* When and how did Canada enter WWII, and
why does the date matter? Name Juno Beach's significance and the
campaign Canada is most remembered for in Europe.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
September 10, 1939 — by vote of Canada's own Parliament, one week
after Britain's declaration; the gap deliberately demonstrated the
independence won by the Statute of Westminster (1914's automatic
entry being the contrast). Juno Beach: the Normandy invasion beach
assigned to Canadian forces on D-Day, June 6, 1944 — one of the five,
alongside two American and two British. The remembered campaign: the
liberation of the Netherlands (1944–45), commemorated by the Dutch
royal tulip gift to Ottawa every year since 1945 and the
Dutch-tended Canadian war graves.
</details>

**Q2.** How did Mackenzie King handle conscription, and why is his
handling judged a success where 1917 was a catastrophe?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
By sequenced ambiguity: an early promise of no overseas conscription;
the 1942 national plebiscite asking release from that promise (Yes 
~64% overall — ~80% in English Canada, ~73% No in Quebec); the
formula "not necessarily conscription, but conscription if
necessary"; and invocation only in November 1944 under real infantry
shortages, sending ~13,000 conscripts of whom ~2,500 reached combat.
Success by comparison: 1917 produced riots, deaths, a party system
split on language lines for generations; 1944 produced resignations
and resentment but no rupture — because King spent five years buying
legitimacy in stages, letting Quebec register dissent (the
plebiscite) without being simply overridden until the necessity was
demonstrable. The takeaway that entered Canadian statecraft:
on French–English existential questions, time-buying ambiguity can
be the highest form of prudence.
</details>

**Q3.** Name the two great home-front injustices of the war era, the
instrument that enabled the first, and each one's eventual redress.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
(1) The internment of ~22,000 Japanese Canadians (1942, under the
War Measures Act) — removal from the BC coast, confiscation and
forced sale of property, exclusion until 1949, pressured
"repatriation" of thousands: apology and redress package (Mulroney),
1988. (2) The refusal of Jewish refugees fleeing Nazism — emblem:
turning away the MS St. Louis's 907 passengers in 1939 (254 later
murdered in the Holocaust), under the "None is too many" policy
climate: formal apology (Trudeau), 2018. Both now standard content
in Canadian schooling and citizenship materials — the country's
practice of documented, delayed apology (Head Tax 2006, Komagata
Maru 2016, residential schools 2008) becoming almost an institution
itself, which Lesson 24 examines.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-plebiscite"></a>**plebiscite** (PLEB-ih-site) — a direct national yes/no vote on one question (similar to a referendum).
- <a id="w-convoy"></a>**convoy** — merchant ships sailing together under warship escort; <a id="w-u-boat"></a>**U-boat** (YOO-boat) — a German submarine.
- <a id="w-garrison"></a>**garrison** (GAIR-ih-sun) — troops stationed to defend a place; <a id="w-pow"></a>**POW** — prisoner of war.
- <a id="w-war-measures-act"></a>**War Measures Act** — the 1914 emergency law letting cabinet rule by decree, suspending normal rights (used for both world wars' internments; replaced by the Emergencies Act, 1988).
- <a id="w-confiscate"></a>**confiscate** — for the state to seize property; <a id="w-redress"></a>**redress** (rih-DRESS) — compensation for a past wrong.
- <a id="w-extraterritorial"></a>**extraterritorial** — legally treated as outside the country it is physically in.
- <a id="w-estuary"></a>**estuary** (ESS-choo-air-ee) — the wide mouth of a river where it meets the sea (the Scheldt).
- <a id="w-crown-corporation"></a>**crown corporation** — a company owned by the government (CBC, Canada Post).
- <a id="w-middle-power"></a>**middle power** — a country influential through diplomacy and alliances rather than raw strength — Canada's brand.
- <a id="w-the-holocaust"></a>**the Holocaust** (HOL-oh-kost) — Nazi Germany's murder of six million Jews.
- Names: **Dieppe** (dee-EP), **Juno** (JOO-noh), **Scheldt** (SKELT), **Ortona** (or-TOH-nah), **Caen** (KAHN), **Falaise** (fah-LEZ), **Margriet** (mar-GREET), **Mackenzie King** (muh-KEN-zee), **Mulroney** (mul-ROH-nee), **St. Louis** (saint LOO-iss).
