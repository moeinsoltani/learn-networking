# Lesson 33: The Justice System in Practice

*Words marked ° are explained in plain English in [Words to Know](#words-to-know) at the end of the lesson.*

## Concept

Lesson 30 gave you the courts' architecture; this lesson is the
user's manual — what actually happens when ordinary life touches
the system: a traffic stop, an arrest, a jury **summons**[°](#w-summons), a dispute
with a landlord or contractor. Start with the policing map, which
is genuinely odd:

Policing in Canada is arranged in a way that surprises almost everyone,
including Canadians.

The **RCMP** — the Mounties — handle federal crimes everywhere: organized
crime, national security, and the like. But they also do something no other
national police force does. Under contract, the RCMP *is* the provincial
police force in eight provinces and all three territories, and the local
police in hundreds of towns. The same red-serge federal force is, in most of
the country, your local cop.

Only three provinces run their own **provincial police**: Ontario (the OPP),
Quebec (the SQ), and Newfoundland (the RNC, partially). Big cities run their
own **municipal** forces — Toronto Police Service, Montreal's SPVM,
Vancouver's VPD. And **Indigenous police services** operate on First Nations
under federal-provincial agreements; they are chronically underfunded, and
that is a live political file rather than a historical one.

The upshot: the officer at your car window could answer to any of three
different governments — but the law being enforced, the Criminal Code, is
identical across the country, because criminal law is federal (Lesson 29).

And one framing honesty, carried from Lessons 17 and 24: the
system's fairness is real *and unevenly distributed* — Indigenous
and Black Canadians are stopped, charged, and jailed at rates the
system's own commissions call systemic discrimination. Both facts
belong in the manual.

## Going Deeper

### Your rights on arrest or detention (the s.10 script)

If detained or arrested, the Charter (Lesson 31) entitles you to:
**be told why, promptly** (s.10(a)); **counsel without delay** —
police must tell you this, provide a phone, and stop questioning
until you've had a reasonable chance to call (s.10(b); free duty
counsel exists 24/7 in every province); **silence** (s.7 — beyond
identifying yourself in specified situations, you need not answer;
notably, unlike US Miranda practice, *your lawyer cannot sit in
the interrogation room* — you consult, then face questioning
alone, and police may continue asking after you've consulted);
**bail without unreasonable delay** (s.11(e) — appearance before
a justice within 24 hours, release the default). Practical script
any lawyer gives: identify yourself where required, say "I want
to speak to a lawyer," then say nothing else — politely,
indefinitely. Evidence obtained through Charter breaches gets
excluded where admission would disrepute justice (s.24(2)) —
the remedy that makes the script matter.

### Criminal court flow (what ~95% actually looks like)

Charge → first appearance (provincial court — scheduling, not
trial) → Crown discloses evidence (full **disclosure**[°](#w-disclosure) is
constitutional: *Stinchcombe*) → resolution discussions: the
system runs on **guilty pleas and withdrawals** (~90% of charges
never see trial — plea bargaining exists, Canadian-style: less
lurid than American, same economics) → if trial: provincial court
(judge alone) for most; serious **indictable offences**[°](#w-indictable-offence) offer
elections up to superior court with judge-and-jury → sentencing
with *Gladue* (Lesson 30) where applicable → appeal rights.
Timeline discipline: *Jordan*'s 18/30-month ceilings (Lesson 30). Legal aid: provincial plans cover the poorest facing
jail — thresholds so low that the working poor routinely
self-represent, which is the access-to-justice crisis in one
clause.

### Jury duty: the summons decoded

Provinces run juries from citizen rolls (citizens only —
Lesson 32; sourced from health-card/voter lists). The flow:
**summons** (a questionnaire + reporting date — ignoring it is an
offence) → **selection day** (hundreds wait; panels called;
challenges: for-cause plus judicial stand-asides — peremptory
challenges were **abolished in 2019** after the Gerald Stanley
case, where an all-white jury acquitted in the killing of a young
Cree man, Colten Boushie — reform driven directly by Lesson 24's
file) → if selected: **12 jurors**, criminal verdicts
**unanimous**; civil juries (rarer, 6–8, majority rules) →
exemptions/deferrals: prior service, hardship, some occupations
(police, lawyers); employers must give (mostly unpaid) leave —
juror pay is provincial and famously meagre (from $0 for early
days in Ontario to modest per diems — a real regressive burden,
much criticized). The strict rule newcomers must hear:
**Canadian jurors are permanently forbidden from disclosing
deliberations** (Criminal Code s.649 — no tell-all interviews
ever; contrast the American post-verdict press scrum), and
researching the case yourself is contempt. Serve if you can:
the jury is the one state function performed entirely by
citizens, the courtroom's answer to Phase 5's civic-culture
dependency.

### Civil life's actual venues

- **Small claims court** (up to $35k Ontario, $15–20k elsewhere):
  simplified forms, no lawyer needed, filing fees ~$100-range;
  the deposit, the botched renovation, the unpaid invoice.
  Realism: winning is easier than *collecting* — judgments
  aren't cheques, and enforcement (**garnishment**[°](#w-garnishment), **liens**[°](#w-lien)) is the
  loser-pays-eventually part nobody warns you about.
- **Tribunals** (Lesson 30): landlord–tenant, employment
  standards, human rights (discrimination in housing/work/
  services — the *private*-sphere complement to the Charter's
  state-only reach, Lesson 31), workers' comp, immigration and
  refugee. Cheaper, faster-ish (some, like Ontario's LTB, run
  notorious backlogs), designed for self-representation.
- **Police complaints**: every jurisdiction has civilian
  oversight (Ontario's **SIU**[°](#w-siu) for deaths/serious injury — the
  model), of varying and much-debated independence.
- **Human-rights codes**: federal + 13 provincial/territorial —
  protected grounds (race, religion, sex, disability, sexual
  orientation, gender identity...) in employment, housing,
  services; remedies compensatory, not criminal. This is where
  "can my landlord/boss do that?" usually lands.

{: .note }
> **The two-truths file (read before forming takes)**
> Truth one: by global standards Canadian justice is
> independent, non-corrupt, procedurally serious — police don't
> take bribes, judges aren't bought, verdicts follow evidence.
> Truth two: its outcomes skew — Indigenous people at ~30%+ of
> federal custody on ~5% of population (L.24), Black Canadians
> overrepresented in street checks and custody, Indigenous
> policing underfunded relative to neighbours, and the Stanley
> verdict's aftermath was a national wound, not a fringe
> complaint. Royal commissions have said "systemic" since the
> 1990s (Manitoba's Aboriginal Justice Inquiry) — the system's
> own paperwork concedes it. Carry both truths; either alone
> is propaganda.

## Checkpoint

**Q1.** *(citizenship-style)* Name four rights you have on arrest
in Canada, and the two ways Canadian practice differs from what
American TV taught you.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Rights: to be promptly told the reason (s.10(a)); to counsel
without delay, with police informing you and facilitating the
call — free 24/7 duty counsel exists (s.10(b)); to silence
(s.7); to reasonable bail and appearance before a justice
within 24 hours (s.11(e)); plus exclusion of evidence from
serious breaches (s.24(2)). TV differences: (1) no lawyer in
the interrogation room — you consult by phone/in private, then
face questioning alone, and police may keep asking after; the
right is to advice, which you must keep applying; (2) no
Miranda-style recitation-or-release magic — and on the other
side, roadside breath demands are mandatory and refusal is
itself an offence. (Bonus: jury deliberations are permanently
secret — no post-verdict interviews.)
</details>

**Q2.** Who might be policing you in Canada and why is the RCMP's
role unusual? Include the Indigenous policing note.

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Depending on where you stand: a municipal force (big cities),
a provincial force (Ontario's OPP, Quebec's SQ), or the RCMP —
which is unusual in being simultaneously the federal police
AND, by contract, the provincial/local police for eight
provinces, the territories, and hundreds of municipalities: a
national force doing small-town traffic stops, with recurring
debates about whether contract policing serves either function
well. First Nations police services operate under federal–
provincial agreements and are chronically underfunded relative
to comparable forces — declared an essential service in
principle, litigated in practice — one of Lesson 24's
present-tense files.
</details>

**Q3.** What changed in Canadian jury selection in 2019, what
case drove it, and what does the episode illustrate about how
the system reforms?

**Your answer:**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>
<br>
Parliament abolished peremptory challenges (vetoes without
stated reason) after the 2018 Gerald Stanley trial, where the
defence used them to exclude visibly Indigenous jurors and an
all-white jury acquitted Stanley in the shooting death of
Colten Boushie, a young Cree man — a verdict that produced
national protest and direct federal legislation within months.
Illustration: the Canadian reform loop — tragedy → public
reckoning → statutory fix (compare Jordan on delay, the
Emergencies Act after 1970, redress after internment): the
system does self-correct, characteristically after the harm
and through ordinary legislation rather than constitutional
change; and the driver here was Lesson 24's larger file —
Indigenous confidence in justice — of which jury composition
was one visible, fixable fragment.
</details>

## Words to Know

*Simple definitions and pronunciations for the terms marked ° above.*

- <a id="w-detention"></a>**detention** — being held by police without being free to leave (short of arrest).
- <a id="w-duty-counsel"></a>**duty counsel** — the free, on-call lawyer available 24/7 to anyone arrested.
- <a id="w-disclosure"></a>**disclosure** — the Crown's obligation to show the accused *all* its evidence before trial.
- <a id="w-plea-bargain-guilty-plea"></a>**plea bargain / guilty plea** — resolving a charge by agreement instead of trial.
- <a id="w-indictable-offence"></a>**indictable offence** (in-DYE-tah-bul — the "c" is silent) — a serious criminal charge; lesser ones are "summary" offences.
- <a id="w-summons"></a>**summons** (SUM-unz) — an official order to appear (e.g., for jury duty).
- <a id="w-peremptory-challenge"></a>**peremptory challenge** (per-EMP-tor-ee) — rejecting a potential juror without giving any reason (abolished in Canada, 2019).
- <a id="w-deliberations"></a>**deliberations** — the jury's private discussions; <a id="w-contempt-of-court"></a>**contempt of court** — disobeying or disrespecting the court's rules (a punishable offence).
- <a id="w-garnishment"></a>**garnishment** — collecting a court judgment by taking money directly from the loser's wages or bank account; <a id="w-lien"></a>**lien** (LEEN) — a legal claim registered against property until a debt is paid.
- <a id="w-carding-street-check"></a>**carding / street check** — police stopping and documenting people not suspected of any crime.
- <a id="w-civilian-oversight"></a>**civilian oversight** — independent non-police bodies that investigate police conduct.
- Names: **Stinchcombe** (STINCH-kum), **Colten Boushie** (BOO-shee), <a id="w-siu"></a>**SIU** — Ontario's Special Investigations Unit.
